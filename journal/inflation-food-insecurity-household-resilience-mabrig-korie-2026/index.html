Inflation, Food Insecurity, and Household Resilience: An Empirical Analysis of the Cost-of-Living Crisis in Nigeria

Abstract

Nigeria has endured one of the most punishing cost-of-living crises in its recent history. The abrupt removal of the Premium Motor Spirit subsidy in May 2023, followed by the unification and float of the exchange rate, triggered immediate and compounding shocks that ripped through domestic markets. Food prices climbed faster than wages or informal earnings could adjust, pushing millions of households into acute food insecurity. This paper follows those macroeconomic decisions all the way down to the household level—documenting reduced dietary diversity, the steady depletion of assets, and the turn toward increasingly destructive coping strategies. Drawing on Amartya Sen's entitlement theory and the sustainable livelihoods framework, the analysis explains why physical food availability has not translated into real access for large sections of the population. Using the latest data from the National Bureau of Statistics, FEWS NET, IPC analyses, IFPRI reports, and recent micro-level surveys, the paper maps the scale of dietary degradation, stark regional variations in vulnerability, and the sequence of household responses. It highlights persistent weaknesses in Nigeria's social protection architecture, especially around targeting accuracy, coverage gaps, and the yawning distance between temporary palliatives and structural solutions. The paper closes with a focused set of short-term and medium-term policy recommendations aimed at lowering transport pass-through costs, strengthening the Social Register with better verification, and investing in the rural infrastructure and smallholder productivity that ultimately determine whether food remains affordable. Nigeria's experience carries clear lessons for other countries attempting subsidy reform under conditions of high inflation and fragile institutions.
