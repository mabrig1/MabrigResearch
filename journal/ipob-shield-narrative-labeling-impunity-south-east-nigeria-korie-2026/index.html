Abstract

Since the 2017 proscription of the Indigenous People of Biafra (IPOB) and the emergence of its armed wing, the Eastern Security Network (ESN), Nigeria’s South-East geopolitical zone has experienced a protracted security crisis marked by attacks on security personnel, political assassinations, communal violence, and a documented pattern of state response that this article terms the IPOB shield: the routine, evidentially unsupported attribution of unsolved killings, disputed deaths, and politically inconvenient violence to IPOB/ESN by police and military authorities, typically within hours of an incident and prior to any meaningful investigation. Synthesizing a June 2026 Sahara Reporters investigative exclusive, Amnesty International’s decade-spanning documentation (2021, 2022, 2025), Premium Times’ 2022 investigation of Nigerian Army practice, court records, and corroborated family testimony, this article traces the shield’s operation across two security institutions — the Nigeria Police Force and the Nigerian Army — from the 2017 Operation Python Dance raids through the ongoing Operation Udo Ka, with the February 2023 assassination of Labour Party senatorial candidate Chief Oyibo Chukwu in Enugu as its central evidentiary case.

The article advances a theoretical contribution beyond the underlying journalism: it situates the IPOB shield within the sociological literatures on moral panic (Cohen, 1972) and labeling (Becker, 1963; Goffman, 1963), arguing that the persistence of narrative foreclosure across both the police and military patterns documented here is best explained not as isolated institutional failure but as a recurring organizational practice through which a contested public label is deployed to substitute classification for investigation. Drawing a comparative line to Human Rights Watch’s documentation of the October 2020 Lekki tollgate shooting and its aftermath, the article argues that narrative foreclosure of this kind is a generalizable feature of unaccountable security governance in Nigeria, not a phenomenon confined to one region or one proscribed organization. While acknowledging the real and lethal violence committed by armed actors operating under the IPOB/ESN banner, and the legitimate security interests of the Nigerian state, the article concludes that the over-application of a single label has functioned to deny justice to victims regardless of who killed them, and proposes a six-point accountability and reform agenda grounded in independent judicial inquiry, mandatory forensic protocol, and transparent operational reporting.

1. Introduction: The Crisis of Narrative and Accountability

Nigeria’s South-East geopolitical zone has endured a protracted and multidimensional security crisis since at least 2017, marked by attacks on security forces, political assassinations, communal violence, kidnappings, and human rights allegations directed at state and non-state actors alike. The 2017 federal proscription of the Indigenous People of Biafra (IPOB) as an unlawful organization, and the subsequent emergence of its armed wing, the Eastern Security Network (ESN), intensified confrontations between separatist-aligned actors and state security agencies. Successive military operations — from Operation Python Dance (2016–2017) through the ongoing Operation Udo Ka — have been publicly framed as proportionate responses to terrorism and to attacks by so-called “unknown gunmen.”

A growing body of investigative journalism and human rights documentation, however, reveals a counter-pattern that this article terms the IPOB shield: the routine, swift, and frequently evidentially unsupported attribution of violent incidents, unsolved murders, and disputed deaths to IPOB or ESN, in a manner that forecloses rather than initiates investigation. This practice, as the evidence assembled below documents, performs several institutional functions simultaneously. It supplies a ready-made explanation that allows a case file to close with minimal further inquiry. It retroactively justifies reprisal operations and the use of lethal force. It deflects scrutiny from political, criminal, or institutional explanations that might implicate actors closer to the state itself. And it shields perpetrators — whether non-state criminal actors, politically motivated assassins, or state agents acting outside the law — from the accountability that an impartial investigation would otherwise impose.

The empirical anchor for this article is a June 18, 2026 exclusive investigation by Sahara Reporters, EXCLUSIVE: How Police Use IPOB Narrative To Bury Unsolved Killings, Cover Up Wrongful Arrests, Others In South-East Nigeria (Sahara Reporters, 2026), which documented the pattern at the level of the Nigeria Police Force across several emblematic cases. This article extends that investigation in three respects. First, it situates the police-level pattern within a parallel and frequently more lethal pattern of post-hoc IPOB/ESN labeling documented in Nigerian Army operations from 2017 to the present, drawing on Amnesty International’s multi-year documentation (Amnesty International, 2021, 2022, 2025) and Premium Times’ 2022 investigative reporting (Premium Times, 2022). Second, it develops a theoretical account of why the pattern recurs and persists, drawing on the sociology of moral panic (Cohen, 1972) and labeling theory (Becker, 1963; Goffman, 1963) to argue that narrative foreclosure functions here as an organizational practice rather than a series of unrelated lapses. Third, it situates the South-East pattern comparatively against Human Rights Watch’s documentation of the October 2020 Lekki tollgate shooting (Human Rights Watch, 2021), arguing that narrative control of this kind is a more general feature of Nigerian security governance under conditions of weak external accountability, not a phenomenon unique to one region or one proscribed organization.

The stakes of this inquiry extend beyond any single case. Impunity of the kind documented below corrodes public trust in security institutions, fuels the cycles of grievance and radicalization that sustain the underlying conflict, and undermines the constitutional guarantee that every Nigerian citizen, regardless of region or political affiliation, is owed equal protection of the law and an impartial investigation when that protection fails. Families of the dead and detained — whether of a senatorial candidate, a custodial death, or a community raid — are owed an investigation, not a label. This article compiles the documented evidence, develops a systemic and theoretical account of why the pattern persists, and closes with a concrete accountability agenda.

1.1 Scope, Sources, and Methodology

This article employs a qualitative document-synthesis methodology, drawing on five categories of source material: (a) the full text of the Sahara Reporters investigation of June 18, 2026, including its documented case files and named expert interviews; (b) Amnesty International’s decade-spanning South-East documentation, principally A Decade of Impunity: Attacks and Unlawful Killings in South-East Nigeria (Amnesty International, 2025), which records 1,844 killings between January 2021 and June 2023 alone, together with Amnesty’s August 2021 report on a documented four-month surge in security-force killings (Amnesty International, 2021) and its July 2022 statement on the Ebubeagu-linked killing of seven youths in Awomamma, Imo State (Amnesty International, 2022); (c) Premium Times’ September 2022 investigative report on Nigerian Army practice during anti-ESN raids (Premium Times, 2022); (d) corroborating reporting from independent Nigerian outlets — Vanguard, Daily Post, Punch, allAfrica, TheNiche, and thesun.ng — on individually named cases; and (e) court records and public statements from the Nigeria Police Force, the Nigerian Army, and the Abia State judiciary, included for balance and where they bear directly on disputed facts.

Three limitations are acknowledged at the outset. First, classified security documents, complete forensic and autopsy records, and internal disciplinary files are not publicly accessible, and the analysis is accordingly built from public investigative, human rights, and judicial sources rather than from primary access to security-sector records. Second, many of the cases documented below remain formally unresolved, with families denied access to investigation files or, in several instances, to the bodies of the deceased; this article reports the documented status of each case as of the most recent available reporting rather than asserting a final determination of culpability. Third, where official counter-narratives exist — police or military statements attributing a death to a “shoot-out” with IPOB/ESN combatants, for example — they are reported alongside the disputing evidence rather than omitted, consistent with this article’s argument that the central problem is not that every official attribution is false, but that very few are subjected to the independent verification that would establish whether they are true.

1.2 Terminology: Why “Shield” Rather Than “Bias”

The choice of terminology matters for what the argument that follows does and does not claim. Describing the pattern documented in this article as institutional “bias” would imply a primarily attitudinal failure — security personnel who think poorly of IPOB and ESN and therefore over-attribute violence to them — correctable chiefly through training or messaging. The evidence reviewed in Sections 3 and 4 supports a stronger and more specific claim: that the IPOB/ESN attribution functions, in the documented cases, as an institutional shield, in the sense developed in the organizational-deviance literature of a routine practice that protects the institution applying it from the costs — investigative, political, or reputational — that a fuller inquiry would impose, independent of whether the individual officer applying the label privately believes it. A shield, unlike a bias, is functional for the institution that deploys it regardless of the sincerity of the belief behind any single instance of its use, which is precisely why, as Section 5 argues, training-based interventions alone are unlikely to dislodge it.

This distinction also clarifies what would, and would not, falsify the article’s central claim. A single case of accurate IPOB/ESN attribution, even a politically convenient one, would not falsify the narrative-shield hypothesis; the hypothesis predicts a pattern across cases in which attribution precedes evidence and forecloses rather than triggers independent verification, not that every individual attribution is necessarily false. Sections 3 and 4 are accordingly structured to test the pattern across multiple, independently sourced cases rather than to rest the argument on any single instance.

2. Theoretical Framework: Moral Panic, Labeling, and the Sociology of Institutional Shields

The recurrence of the same narrative pattern across two distinct security institutions, multiple states, and nearly a decade invites a theoretical account of why classification so consistently substitutes for investigation. This article draws on three established sociological literatures to construct that account: moral panic theory, labeling theory, and the sociology of stigma.

2.1 Moral Panic and the Construction of a Folk Devil

Stanley Cohen’s foundational study Folk Devils and Moral Panics (Cohen, 1972) describes how societies periodically identify a group as a disproportionate threat to social order, a process in which the media, control agencies, and “right-thinking people” converge to amplify the group’s deviance and to treat its members as a homogeneous, dangerous category requiring exceptional response. Cohen’s concept of the folk devil — a visible, named, and morally simplified target onto which diffuse social anxiety is condensed — describes with some precision the public status IPOB and ESN have occupied in South-East Nigerian security discourse since 2017. Once a group has been constituted as a folk devil, Cohen observes, control agencies gain a standing justification for expanded enforcement powers and reduced evidentiary scrutiny, because action against the folk devil is pre-legitimated by the panic itself, independent of the specific facts of any given incident.

Applied to the cases documented in Sections 3 and 4, the moral panic framework explains a feature of the evidence that a purely case-by-case account would treat as coincidental: the consistency with which security spokespersons reach for an IPOB/ESN attribution within hours of an incident, before any forensic or investigative basis for that attribution could plausibly exist. This is precisely the pattern Cohen’s framework predicts once a folk devil has been constituted — the label is available for deployment prior to, and independent of, the evidence a specific case would require to support it.

2.2 Labeling Theory and the Power to Define Deviance

Howard Becker’s Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance (Becker, 1963) argues that deviance is not an inherent property of an act or a person but a status conferred by those with the institutional power to apply the label successfully — a power Becker locates specifically in agencies of social control such as police forces, whose labeling decisions carry consequences regardless of their evidentiary basis. Becker’s framework directs attention to an asymmetry that recurs throughout the cases below: the entity applying the IPOB/ESN label — a police spokesperson, an army press statement — typically possesses both the institutional authority to make the label stick in public discourse and the practical ability to forestall the kind of independent investigation that could contest it. The labeled individual, by contrast, is frequently deceased, in custody, or otherwise unable to contest the classification through ordinary channels, a structural imbalance that helps explain why so few of the attributions documented here have been formally challenged or overturned despite credible disputing evidence.

2.3 Stigma and the Discrediting of Victims

Erving Goffman’s Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (Goffman, 1963) describes how a discrediting attribute, once publicly assigned, reorganizes how a person’s entire biography and circumstances are subsequently read by others — a process Goffman terms the management of a “spoiled identity.” Once a victim has been publicly labeled an IPOB or ESN member or sympathizer, Goffman’s framework predicts, that label retroactively colors how the victim’s death, the family’s grievance, and any disputing evidence are subsequently received by the public, the press, and investigating authorities — a discrediting effect independent of whether the label was accurate. This dynamic appears repeatedly below: family testimony disputing an official attribution is treated, in much of the documented reporting, as the predictable defensiveness of relatives of a presumed combatant rather than as potentially probative evidence, even where, as in the Thaddeus Ojokoh case examined in Section 3.2, the disputing evidence was subsequently substantiated.

2.4 Synthesis: The Narrative Shield as Institutional Practice

Read together, these three frameworks support a synthesis this article terms the narrative shield: a recurring institutional practice in which a pre-constituted folk devil (Cohen, 1972) is invoked through an asymmetric labeling power (Becker, 1963) that, once applied, discredits the victim’s case for further scrutiny (Goffman, 1963), with the cumulative effect of substituting public classification for the investigative process that due process and constitutional rights of the citizen would otherwise require. This synthesis generates a testable prediction that distinguishes it from a simple narrative of institutional incompetence: narrative-shield cases should show attribution preceding evidence rather than following it, and should show disputing evidence treated as discreditable rather than investigable. Sections 3 and 4 examine the documented police and military patterns against this prediction; in the cases for which sufficiently detailed reporting exists — the Oyibo Chukwu assassination, the Thaddeus Ojokoh case, and the September 2017 Afaraukwu raid — the prediction is borne out.

A further implication of this synthesis concerns the institutions, rather than the individuals, that sustain the pattern. Becker’s account of labeling power locates the capacity to make a deviance label stick specifically in agencies of social control, not in any single official; the persistence of the narrative shield across changes in personnel, across two distinct security institutions, and across nearly a decade is therefore more consistent with an organizational practice embedded in incentive structures — the absence of a costed consequence for an unverified attribution, and the presence of a costed consequence for a prolonged unresolved case — than with a deliberate conspiracy among individual officers. This reframing matters directly for the reform agenda developed in Section 8, which targets institutional incentive and reporting structures rather than individual retraining alone.

3. The Police Pattern: Evidence from the Sahara Reporters Investigation

On June 18, 2026, Sahara Reporters published an exclusive investigation documenting how the Nigeria Police Force in the South-East has repeatedly attributed violent crimes, political assassinations, and disputed deaths to IPOB/ESN, in several documented instances within hours of the precipitating incident and prior to any disclosed forensic or investigative basis for the attribution (Sahara Reporters, 2026). The investigation argues that this practice has, in multiple documented cases, foreclosed inquiry into politically or criminally motivated violence and obscured wrongful arrests and deaths in custody.

3.1 Central Case: The Assassination of Chief Oyibo Chukwu

The Sahara Reporters investigation centers on the February 22, 2023 assassination of Chief Oyibo Chukwu, a Labour Party senatorial candidate for the Enugu East Senatorial District and former chairman of the Nigerian Bar Association’s Oji River branch. Gunmen ambushed Chukwu and his aides at Amechi Awkunanaw in Enugu South Local Government Area, days before Nigeria’s 2023 general elections, and set the vehicle ablaze.

Within hours, the Enugu State Police Command, through spokesperson DSP Daniel Ndukwe, publicly attributed the killing to “IPOB/ESN renegades” and “subversive criminal elements suspected to be IPOB/ESN renegades,” without public presentation of supporting evidence. The victim’s family, led by his younger brother, Chief Lucky Chukwu, rejected the attribution and alleged a political motive, noting that a combined team of soldiers and mobile policemen previously guarding the axis had been withdrawn the day before the killing — a fact independently corroborated in subsequent reporting. As of this writing, more than three years after the killing, the case remains formally unresolved, with the family alleging that the investigation was effectively abandoned once the IPOB/ESN attribution was issued. A second brother, Kelvin Chukwu, has since pursued his own Labour Party candidacy, a documented fact that underscores the family’s continued public engagement with the case rather than withdrawal from it.

Expert commentary collected in the Sahara Reporters investigation reinforces the pattern this article analyzes theoretically in Section 2. Retired Chief Superintendent of Police and lawyer Chidi Ezenwa is quoted: “When a police force becomes more political than practical, they move away from their core values… almost every violent incident became attributed to IPOB activities.” Ezenwa further observed that a proper investigation of the Chukwu case would likely trace to political and community interests, noting that “IPOB [has] no known issue with these individuals.” Rights and Liberty Advocacy Center (RULAAC) Director Okechukwu Nwanguma and Nigerian Bar Association President Afam Osigwe, SAN, are separately quoted on how a weaponized security narrative erodes community trust and allows the actual perpetrators of political violence to escape scrutiny.

3.2 A Broader Pattern: Wrongful Arrests, Custodial Deaths, and Abandoned Cases

The Sahara Reporters investigation documents a series of additional cases that, considered together with the Chukwu case, establish the broader pattern this article analyzes. Onuoha Johnbosco was arrested at his printing shop in Imo State by operatives posing as customers; police initially denied holding him in custody, then later stated he had died in a gun battle as an IPOB operative, an account disputed by his family and by eyewitnesses to the arrest. Thaddeus Ikechukwu Ojokoh was publicly paraded by security operatives as responsible for an attack on police personnel in Ngor Okpala Local Government Area, Imo State, in April 2023; subsequent evidence, independently reported by Vanguard, allAfrica, and the Daily Review Online, established that Ojokoh was already in police custody at the time the attack he was paraded for occurred. The case against him was reportedly abandoned only after sustained public pressure and a formal RULAAC petition to the Inspector-General of Police.

Japheth Njoku died in detention at the “Tiger Base” police facility in Owerri, Imo State, between March and May 2025; police attributed the death to illness, while the family alleged extortion — reporting by Sahara Reporters, TheNiche, and thesun.ng documents a specific demand of approximately ₦4 million and names a named officer, Inspector Barnabas, in connection with the family’s allegations — and denial of access to Njoku prior to his death. A coroner reportedly ordered an autopsy, which the family states was not produced. Chief Dons Udeh was abducted in April 2023 and later found dead near the Ninth Mile area of Enugu State, with his phone reportedly recovered at the Ogui Police Station; reporting by Sahara Reporters, Daily Post, and Punch documents the case as stalled following an initial IPOB attribution, with no subsequent public resolution.

Sahara Reporters’ synthesis attributes the pattern to a combination of weak investigative capacity, political pressure, the deployment of officers unfamiliar with local terrain, and a default reliance on the IPOB/ESN attribution as a substitute for investigation. Ezenwa’s assessment, quoted in the original investigation, is direct: “Policing is local. You need officers who understand the terrain, culture, and people. Otherwise, you misread conflicts and rely on lazy narratives.”

4. The Military Pattern: Operations, Reprisals, and Retroactive Labeling

While the Sahara Reporters investigation centers on police practice, a parallel and in several documented instances more lethal pattern has been independently established for the Nigerian Army across nearly a decade of South-East operations. Operations publicly framed as counter-insurgency against ESN and IPOB have repeatedly resulted in documented allegations of extrajudicial killing, arbitrary mass arrest, community reprisal, and the post-hoc labeling of victims as combatants to justify the operational outcome.

4.1 Historical Precedent: Operation Python Dance and the Afaraukwu Raid

Operation Python Dance, also known as Egwu Eke (2016–2017), and its expanded second phase launched in September 2017, were military exercises publicly framed as responses to crime and insecurity in the South-East. Amnesty International and several Nigerian and international human rights coalitions documented, during this period, excessive use of force, arbitrary arrests, and killings of unarmed pro-Biafra agitators and supporters. The most consequential single incident occurred between September 12 and 14, 2017, when soldiers raided the Afaraukwu, Abia State, residence of Nnamdi Kanu, the IPOB leader. Casualty estimates for the raid vary by source: the Kanu family’s own account places the number killed at fifteen to more than twenty-eight, a range this article adopts in preference to the vaguer and less independently verifiable estimates of “dozens to over 150” that circulated in some contemporaneous reporting, since the family’s figure has subsequently been treated as the operative basis for litigation.

That litigation produced a documented judicial outcome largely absent from earlier journalistic accounts of the raid: the Abia State High Court subsequently ruled the raid unlawful and ordered the Nigerian Army to pay approximately ₦1 billion in compensation and to issue a public apology to the affected family and community — a judicial finding that corroborates, through an independent institutional channel, the substance of the family’s long-standing account of the raid’s severity and illegality. Amnesty International’s documentation from this period and from later operations consistently notes that many of the dead were labeled IPOB or ESN members after the fact, frequently without public evidence establishing combatant status, and that the practice of treating a “shoot-out” characterization as a substitute for independent investigation dates at least to this period.

4.2 2021: The Documented Surge in Security-Force Killings

Amnesty International’s August 2021 report documented at least 115 people killed by combined police and military action across Imo, Anambra, Abia, and Enugu states in a four-month period, accompanied by more than 500 arrests, many of which Amnesty characterized as random detentions of youths with no credible established connection to ESN (Amnesty International, 2021). In one incident reported in the same period, following the killing of five soldiers and the burning of their vehicle near Enugu communities, soldiers of the Nigerian Army’s 82 Division conducted house-to-house operations in Akpawfu, Amagunze, and Akpugo, arresting at least twenty residents on suspicion of ESN affiliation. Amnesty’s assessment of the broader pattern across this period is direct: “Instead of launching proper investigations into these killings, security and government officials are often quick to claim victims of extrajudicial executions were caught up in shoot-outs or simply label them members of the Eastern Security Network… or unknown gunmen” (Amnesty International, 2021) — a formulation that anticipates, almost verbatim, the police-level pattern subsequently documented by Sahara Reporters in 2026.

4.3 The Ebubeagu Dimension: State-Backed Paramilitaries and the Awomamma Killings

The pattern documented above is not confined to the regular police and army. On July 17, 2022, the state-backed Ebubeagu security outfit was implicated in the killing of seven youths in Awomamma, Oru East Local Government Area, Imo State — an incident that prompted Amnesty International Nigeria’s July 2022 public statement calling for an end to “extrajudicial executions under the guise of tackling insecurity in the South-East” (Amnesty International, 2022). The Awomamma killings illustrate a dimension of the narrative-shield pattern not present in the Chukwu or Afaraukwu cases: a state-sanctioned but locally recruited paramilitary structure, operating with looser institutional accountability than the regular police or army, applying the same retroactive justification template to civilian deaths.

4.4 The Premium Times Investigation: Troops, Unarmed Residents, and Post-Hoc Labeling

A September 2022 Premium Times investigation, How Nigerian Troops Kill Unarmed Residents in South-East, Label Them IPOB Members (Premium Times, 2022), documented in granular case detail how army units conducting raids on suspected ESN camps killed unarmed residents and subsequently labeled the deceased as IPOB or ESN members. Local accounts and family testimony collected in the investigation frequently disputed the official combatant designation. IPOB itself has separately and repeatedly disputed specific army claims — including announced “armoury” discoveries in Enugu — as fabricated; such denials cannot be taken as independently dispositive, but they underscore that even the underlying factual premise of several official attributions has been a matter of public, unresolved dispute rather than settled fact.

4.5 Operation Udo Ka and the Persistence of the Template

The Nigerian Army’s current Operation Udo Ka, launched in 2023 and ongoing, continues the operational template documented in earlier phases: intelligence-led raids, official claims of neutralizing ESN or IPOB fighters, and the recovery of arms and destruction of camps reported through official military communication channels. Official statements emphasize successes against a documented armed threat; the evidence reviewed in this section indicates that allegations of civilian casualties, arbitrary action, and over-attribution have nonetheless persisted across each successive operational phase since 2017, a continuity that is itself evidence against an account that treats each incident as an isolated and unrelated failure.

Considered together, the operational phases reviewed in this section span nearly a decade — from the September 2017 Afaraukwu raid through the ongoing Operation Udo Ka — and three documented institutional actors: the regular Nigerian Army, the Nigeria Police Force examined in Section 3, and the state-backed Ebubeagu paramilitary structure examined in Section 4.3. The consistency of the underlying template across this span — rapid attribution, retroactive combatant labeling, and the absence of independent verification — is itself part of the evidence this article treats as supporting the institutional-practice account developed in Section 2, rather than an account resting on any single operation or unit.

5. Systemic Analysis: Why the Narrative Persists as a Shield

Several interlocking institutional and political factors, identified both by the experts quoted in the Sahara Reporters investigation and by the broader human rights documentation reviewed above, explain why the narrative shield persists as standard practice rather than appearing as an occasional aberration.

Investigative weakness and the economics of the lazy narrative. Security agencies in the South-East frequently lack the forensic capacity, local intelligence networks, and institutional incentive to conduct the kind of thorough, evidence-based investigation a contested death requires. An IPOB/ESN attribution supplies an immediately available, publicly legible explanation that requires minimal further resource commitment to sustain.

Political and institutional pressure for rapid results. High-profile incidents — a political assassination, an attack on soldiers — generate immediate public and institutional pressure for a visible response. Attributing the violence to a federally proscribed organization satisfies that pressure while simultaneously foreclosing inquiry into politically sensitive alternative explanations, including the possibility of political or institutional involvement closer to the state itself.

Retroactive legitimation of reprisal operations. Following attacks on security personnel, reprisal operations against the surrounding community frequently result in civilian casualties. Labeling the dead as IPOB or ESN members after the fact retroactively legitimizes the operation and forecloses the kind of scrutiny an unlabeled civilian death would otherwise attract.

Erosion of community trust and the intelligence feedback loop. Where communities perceive security agencies as biased, predatory, or unaccountable, cooperation with those agencies deteriorates. Diminished community cooperation produces poorer intelligence, which in turn increases reliance on indiscriminate operations and broad-brush narrative attribution — a self-reinforcing cycle documented across each of the operational phases reviewed in Section 4.

A multifaceted conflict reduced to a singular label. Amnesty International’s documentation correctly identifies that the South-East security environment involves multiple, only partially overlapping actors: IPOB and ESN, including internal factions; so-called “unknown gunmen,” some plausibly criminal or politically motivated; cult groups and criminal gangs; vigilante structures; state-backed paramilitaries such as Ebube Agu; and, in the Chukwu case, the possibility of narrowly political violence. Reducing this multifaceted reality to a single proscribed-organization narrative serves the institutional interests identified above, at the direct cost of the targeted, evidence-based response the underlying complexity actually requires.

6. Acknowledging the Full Picture: Legitimate Security Concerns

A rigorous account of the narrative-shield pattern requires explicit acknowledgment of the violence the pattern is invoked to address, which is itself real and documented. IPOB was proscribed in 2017 following activity that Nigerian federal authorities determined posed a threat to national security and public order. Its armed wing, ESN, has been credibly linked, in reporting independent of the cases examined above, to attacks on police stations, correctional facilities, and security personnel across the South-East. Sit-at-home orders enforced through intimidation and, in documented instances, violence have caused substantial economic disruption and a number of civilian deaths. Some individuals operating under the IPOB or ESN banner have been credibly linked to criminality, extortion, and opportunistic violence unrelated to any coherent political program.

Official security operations in the region have also produced genuine outcomes in the public interest: the recovery of weapons, the disruption of armed camps, and, in specific documented instances, the neutralization of individuals credibly linked to attacks on civilians or security personnel. Security personnel killed in the line of duty across this conflict, and their families, are owed the same standard of investigation and recognition this article argues is owed to every other victim named above.

None of this acknowledgment, however, supplies a justification for the documented pattern of narrative substitution, arbitrary detention, or investigative abandonment examined in Sections 3 and 4. Justice for security personnel killed in the line of duty is achieved through the identification and prosecution of their actual killers through due process — a goal the narrative shield, by foreclosing investigation, does not advance and in several documented instances has actively obstructed. Effective security and respect for due process are, on the evidence reviewed in this article, not competing goods to be traded off against one another; they are interdependent conditions for the kind of durable peace neither can separately produce.

It is worth stating directly why this balance matters analytically, beyond fairness to security personnel. A critique of the narrative shield that is read, fairly or not, as a blanket denial of any IPOB/ESN-linked violence would be self-defeating on its own terms: it would hand the institutions whose practice this article documents a ready argument for dismissing the entire critique as one-sided advocacy rather than evidence-based analysis. The more defensible — and, on the evidence reviewed above, more accurate — position is the narrower one this article has tried to sustain throughout: that real armed threats and documented institutional impunity coexist in the South-East, and that the existence of the former does not retroactively validate every instance of the latter.

7. Discussion: A Comparative Pattern Beyond the South-East

The narrative-shield mechanism documented above for IPOB/ESN attribution is not, on the comparative evidence, unique to the South-East or to security responses to separatist organizations specifically. Human Rights Watch’s October 2021 report, Nigeria: A Year On, No Justice for #EndSARS Crackdown (Human Rights Watch, 2021), documents a structurally similar pattern in the aftermath of the October 20, 2020 shooting of #EndSARS protesters at the Lekki tollgate in Lagos: witnesses interviewed by Human Rights Watch reported seeing at least fifteen lifeless bodies at the scene, with the military subsequently reported to have removed at least eleven, while official accounts disputed both the number of casualties and the security forces’ responsibility for them, and a full year after the shooting no security officer had been held criminally accountable.

The comparison is instructive precisely because the Lekki case involves a different security institution, a different geographic region, and a different proscribed-versus-unproscribed status of the protest movement involved, yet exhibits the same structural sequence the theoretical framework in Section 2 predicts: an officially preferred account is advanced rapidly and is not displaced by independent investigation, despite credible disputing evidence, within a timeframe that would ordinarily be expected to produce one. This comparative pattern supports the article’s central theoretical claim — that narrative foreclosure of contested security-force violence is a generalizable institutional practice under conditions of weak external civilian accountability in Nigeria, rather than a phenomenon specific to the IPOB/ESN conflict alone. It also suggests that reforms targeted narrowly at South-East policing or at IPOB/ESN-specific operational guidance would address only one instance of a more general institutional pattern, a point taken up directly in the recommendations that follow.

8. Recommendations for Accountability and Reform

The evidence compiled in this article supports six concrete, sequenced recommendations.

1. Independent judicial inquiry into emblematic cases. Establish transparent, independent inquiry panels, including credible civil society and, where appropriate, international observer participation, to investigate the cases examined in this article — including the Oyibo Chukwu assassination, the disputed custodial deaths of Onuoha Johnbosco and Japheth Njoku, the Thaddeus Ojokoh case, and the specific army operations documented in Section 4 — with a binding requirement of public reporting of findings.

2. Mandatory forensic and investigative standards. Implement mandatory forensic protocols, crime-scene preservation requirements, and independent autopsy access for all deaths occurring during security operations or under disputed circumstances, supported by the deployment of investigators trained in, and where possible drawn from, the local terrain and community in which a death has occurred.

3. Accountability for arbitrary arrest and reprisal operations. Review and, where evidence supports it, prosecute documented cases of arbitrary arrest, torture, and extrajudicial killing identified in this and prior independent reporting. End the practice of mass community raids and collective punishment following attacks on security personnel, and guarantee due process and access to legal representation for all persons detained in connection with IPOB/ESN-linked investigations.

4. Community-oriented policing and intelligence reform. Shift institutional practice from raid-heavy kinetic operations toward intelligence-led, community-anchored policing, supported by local recruitment, cultural-competence training, and genuine investment in community partnership as a means of improving intelligence quality and reducing institutional reliance on broad-brush narrative attribution.

5. Transparent, disaggregated operational reporting. Require public, disaggregated reporting of security operations — casualty figures, the basis for combatant designation, arrest figures, and subsequent legal outcomes — sufficient to permit independent verification and to close the evidentiary gap the narrative shield currently exploits.

6. A holistic response to the conflict’s underlying drivers. Address the conflict’s root drivers — political marginalization grievances, youth unemployment, cultism, and weak local institutions — through development investment, structured dialogue, and inclusive governance, pursued alongside, rather than as a substitute for, targeted and evidentially grounded law enforcement against verifiable criminal and violent actors regardless of the label under which they operate.

9. Implications for Research, Policy Engagement, and Funding

The findings above point to three concrete avenues for follow-on research and applied investment that extend beyond the document-synthesis approach taken in this article. First, an independent, adequately resourced forensic and case-tracking capacity for the South-East — modeled on existing transitional-justice documentation projects elsewhere in Africa — could close the evidentiary gap this article has repeatedly identified as the precondition for the narrative shield’s persistence: cases such as the Chukwu assassination remain unresolved in substantial part because no independent body has had the standing, funding, or forensic capacity to investigate them once the initial police attribution closed the file. Second, a structured, longitudinal dataset disaggregating security-operation casualties by documented combatant status, location, and operating unit — building on Amnesty International’s existing but necessarily episodic reporting — would allow the frequency question this article explicitly leaves open in Section 10 below to be answered with the kind of statistical confidence a single qualitative synthesis cannot supply. Third, comparative research extending the #EndSARS analogy developed in Section 7 to other contested Nigerian security operations — including but not limited to the Boko Haram counter-insurgency in the North-East — could test whether the narrative-shield mechanism identified here generalizes across Nigeria’s several active security theatres or is conditioned on features specific to a proscribed-separatist-organization context.

Each of these avenues is, in the ordinary sense of the term, fundable: each specifies a bounded deliverable (a case-tracking database, a documentation partnership, a comparative coding study), a defensible methodology, and a theory of change linking the research output directly to the accountability reforms proposed in Section 8. Civil society organizations, university human rights centres, and Nigerian and international funders interested in rule-of-law and security-sector accountability programming in West Africa are accordingly positioned to treat this article’s synthesis not only as a finding in its own right but as the evidentiary basis for a specific, scoped grant-funded research and documentation agenda.

10. Limitations of This Study

Three limitations beyond those already noted in Section 1.1 bear directly on how the findings above should be read. First, this article is a qualitative synthesis of publicly available investigative, human rights, and judicial sources, not a primary empirical study with its own dataset of casualty figures or a representative sample of cases; the cases examined in Sections 3 and 4 were selected because they are the cases for which independent, cross-referenced reporting exists in sufficient detail to support analysis, not as a random or comprehensive sample of all disputed security-force attributions in the South-East. The true frequency of the narrative-shield pattern relative to accurate attributions cannot be established from the evidence assembled here, and this article does not claim to establish that frequency.

Second, the theoretical framework developed in Section 2 is offered as an explanatory synthesis of an existing evidentiary record, not as a result independently derived from primary fieldwork, interviews, or institutional access conducted for this article; the moral panic, labeling, and stigma literatures are applied to evidence gathered by others, principally Sahara Reporters, Amnesty International, and Premium Times, whose own methodologies and potential limitations this article has not independently audited beyond cross-referencing their specific factual claims against other independent sources. Third, official Nigerian security institutions have, in the cases reviewed, generally not published a full evidentiary basis for disputed attributions; the analysis above is accordingly built in several instances from the absence of disclosed evidence rather than from a demonstrated falsehood, a distinction this article has tried to preserve in its language throughout but which readers should bear in mind is itself part of what the recommended reforms in Section 8 — particularly mandatory disaggregated reporting — are designed to correct.

11. Conclusion: Justice Demands Evidence, Not Labels

The pattern documented across a 2026 police-level investigation, nearly a decade of Amnesty International monitoring, a 2022 military-level investigation, and the comparative #EndSARS case examined in Section 7 is consistent: the IPOB/ESN narrative has been repeatedly and predictably deployed by Nigerian security institutions as a substitute for the investigation that due process, and the families of the dead, are owed. This conclusion does not depend on denying the existence of armed threats in the South-East or on minimizing the sacrifices of security personnel killed in the line of duty; both are real, and both deserve their own honest accounting.

What the evidence does establish is that sustainable security cannot be built on a narrative practice that consistently sacrifices investigation for classification. Every victim examined in this article — a senatorial candidate ambushed days before an election, a printing-shop owner who died in custody, a man paraded for a crime committed while he was already detained, a household in Afaraukwu whose loss a court has since formally recognized — was owed a thorough, impartial inquiry rather than a label applied within hours and rarely revisited. A label is not evidence. Due process is not optional. And impunity, whether exercised in uniform or justified by the rhetoric of counter-terrorism, corrodes the same institutions it claims to protect.

The path toward a different outcome requires a specific and demonstrable institutional choice: investigating each death on its own evidentiary merits, holding state and non-state perpetrators alike accountable through due process, and treating the narrative convenience of a familiar label as a reason for heightened scrutiny rather than as a substitute for it. Only that choice, consistently applied, can break the cycle of violence, distrust, and foreclosed justice this article has documented.
