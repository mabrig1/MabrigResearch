Manufacturing a Threat: A Critical History of the "Civilizational Jihad" Narrative and Its Divergent Effects on Christian Communities in Nigeria, Europe, and the United States

Abstract

The phrase "civilizational jihad" — sometimes called "stealth jihad" — describes an alleged coordinated strategy to subvert Western institutions from within, using lawful civic participation as cover. This article traces the claim to its specific origin: a single 1991 internal Muslim Brotherhood document entered into evidence in United States v. Holy Land Foundation for Relief and Development (2008), and documents how a small network of authors and advocacy organizations — Robert Spencer, Frank Gaffney's Center for Security Policy, ACT for America, and the drafters of "American Laws for American Courts" model legislation — generalised that document into a sweeping civilizational thesis later embedded in U.S. travel restrictions, state anti-Sharia statutes, and municipal surveillance programs. Drawing on investigative reporting, court records, and peer-reviewed scholarship (Bail, 2015; Center for American Progress, 2011; Esposito & Kalin, 2011; Lean, 2012), the article evaluates what mainstream legal and religious scholarship, and watchdog organizations including the Southern Poverty Law Center and the Council on American-Islamic Relations, find regarding the thesis's evidentiary basis and its documented correlation with rising discrimination against Muslim communities.

Set against this critical history, the article then examines where Christian communities face documented, often severe, persecution: Boko Haram and ISWAP's sustained campaign against churches and Christian villages in northeastern Nigeria (Open Doors, 2024; USCIRF, 2023), the more contested farmer-herder violence of Nigeria's Middle Belt (International Crisis Group, 2017, 2021), episodic violence and church vandalism in parts of Europe, and the largely non-violent, culture-war character of Christian-Muslim tension in the United States. The article argues that collapsing these distinct phenomena into a single "civilizational jihad" framework has produced a double failure: it offers no demonstrated benefit to Nigerian Christians facing actual armed groups, while correlating with documented increases in discrimination against ordinary Muslims in Western democracies. A more defensible response, the article concludes, requires naming each threat with precision rather than substituting a unifying conspiracy for case-specific analysis.

Editorial note: This article takes Christian persecution seriously as a documented, ongoing reality, particularly in Nigeria. It also treats intellectual honesty as a Christian obligation. The two commitments are not in tension: conflating real, location-specific armed violence with an unsubstantiated global conspiracy theory does not protect persecuted Christians, and the evidence reviewed below indicates it has chiefly correlated with discrimination against ordinary Muslims in Western democracies.

1. Introduction: Two Claims That Get Conflated

Two claims circulate together so often in contemporary discourse that they are rarely examined apart. The first is empirical and well documented: in specific places, at specific times, identifiable armed groups have killed, displaced, and terrorised Christian communities — nowhere more severely in recent years than in northeastern and central Nigeria (Open Doors, 2024; USCIRF, 2023). The second is a sweeping interpretive framework, often called "civilizational jihad" or "stealth jihad," which holds that this violence is one expression of a single, coordinated, multi-generational strategy — extending into university diversity offices, corporate boardrooms, British family-arbitration tribunals, and American voting blocs — to "destroy Western civilization from within" (Akram, 1991, as cited in U.S. Department of Justice, 2008).

This article separates the two claims and examines each on its own evidentiary footing. Section 2 traces the second claim to its specific textual origin and asks what that source document actually establishes. Section 3 identifies the authors and organizations who built the broader thesis on top of it. Section 4 documents how the thesis shaped concrete law and policy in the United States. Section 5 reviews what mainstream legal scholarship, religious studies scholarship, and civil-rights watchdog organizations conclude about its accuracy and its measured social effects. Section 6 returns to the first claim — genuine, documented anti-Christian violence — and examines it separately in Nigeria, Europe, and the United States, each of which presents a materially different picture. Section 7 discusses the cost of treating these as one phenomenon, and Section 8 concludes with an argument for precision over conflation as the more defensible — and more genuinely protective — response for Christian communities under real threat.

The approach taken here follows ordinary standards of evidence: a claim's age, its repetition across partisan media, or its emotional resonance with real suffering are not substitutes for what courts, peer-reviewed scholarship, and independent investigative reporting can establish. Measured against those standards, the article finds that the "civilizational jihad" framework, as a unified global thesis, does not hold up — while the specific, narrower claim that Boko Haram and ISWAP are waging a documented campaign of violence against Nigerian Christians holds up entirely.

2. Origins: The 1991 Memorandum and Its Evidentiary Weight

2.1 The Holy Land Foundation Trial

The textual root of the "civilizational jihad" concept is a single internal document: the Explanatory Memorandum on the General Strategic Goal for the Group in North America, attributed to Mohamed Akram and dated 1991 (Akram, 1991). The memorandum surfaced publicly as evidence in United States v. Holy Land Foundation for Relief and Development, a federal prosecution of a Texas-based charity for funnelling money to Hamas, a U.S.-designated terrorist organization. The prosecution secured convictions in 2008 on charges of providing material support to a terrorist organization — a real and significant outcome (U.S. Department of Justice, 2008; Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2009).

The memorandum itself describes Brotherhood-affiliated work in North America as a form of "settlement," and states that members should regard their work as "a kind of grand Jihad in eliminating and destroying the Western civilization from within" (Akram, 1991). It names a list of Brotherhood-linked organizations operating in the United States at the time it was written.

2.2 What the Memo Does and Does Not Establish

Two distinctions matter for evaluating what this document can responsibly support. First, the memorandum was introduced at trial to help establish organizational intent and financial ties within a specific charity-financing network connected to Hamas — not as a finding, by the court or by federal investigators, that it describes an operative program controlling the broad landscape of American Muslim civic life (CAIR, n.d.; Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2009). The conviction the memo helped support was for material support to a designated terrorist group, a narrower legal finding than "civilizational subversion."

Second, no subsequent court ruling, congressional investigation, or intelligence assessment has established that the memorandum reflects an ongoing, centrally coordinated plan binding the world's approximately 1.9 billion Muslims, or even the much smaller population of American Muslim civic and religious organizations, the overwhelming majority of which have no documented connection to the Brotherhood network named in the 1991 text. The leap from "one internal planning memo, introduced as evidence in one financing prosecution" to "a unified, multi-decade global strategy against Western civilization" was not made by the courts. It was made afterward, by a specific and traceable set of authors and advocacy organizations, examined next.

3. Who Built the "Civilizational Jihad" Framework

3.1 Robert Spencer and Stealth Jihad

Robert Spencer, who operates the website Jihad Watch, did more than any other single author to popularise the broader thesis as a mass-market political concept. His book Stealth Jihad: How Radical Islam Is Subverting America without Guns or Bombs (Spencer, 2008) argued that routine activity by Muslim civil-rights organizations — litigation, lobbying, public advocacy — was itself evidence of a coordinated subversion campaign, treating the existence of such activity as proof of the thesis rather than as ordinary participation in a pluralist democracy.

3.2 Frank Gaffney and the Center for Security Policy

Frank Gaffney's Center for Security Policy published Shariah: The Threat to America (Center for Security Policy, 2010), authored by a self-styled "Team B" with no specialist background in Islamic jurisprudence, arguing that Islamic religious practice in the United States constituted an inherent national-security threat. The Southern Poverty Law Center has documented Gaffney's subsequent promotion of the unsubstantiated claim that a senior U.S. political aide held secret Muslim Brotherhood ties, and designates his organization an anti-Muslim extremist group (SPLC, 2015; SPLC, n.d.-a).

3.3 ACT for America and Model Anti-Sharia Legislation

ACT for America, founded by Brigitte Gabriel, became the largest grassroots organization built around the framework, lobbying state legislatures and Congress; the Southern Poverty Law Center designates it the largest anti-Muslim hate group operating in the United States (SPLC, n.d.-b). Attorney David Yerushalmi drafted the model legislation known as "American Laws for American Courts" (ALAC), worded to bar state courts from considering "foreign law" in terms that both its drafters and independent analysts agree were designed specifically to target Islamic religious law (Center for American Progress, 2011).

3.4 Fear, Inc. and the Funding Network

The 2011 investigative report Fear, Inc.: The Roots of the Islamophobia Network in America traced more than $40 million in funding from seven foundations to this same small cluster of authors and organizations, documenting how the same claims were recirculated between think tanks, opinion media, and state legislators to create an appearance of independent corroboration where the underlying sourcing was, in fact, highly concentrated (Center for American Progress, 2011). The sociologist Christopher Bail's later academic study Terrified: How Anti-Muslim Fringe Organizations Became Mainstream (Bail, 2015) reached a structurally similar conclusion through citation-network analysis: a small number of fringe organizations achieved disproportionate influence over post-9/11 media coverage by supplying emotionally charged content that outcompeted more measured statements from mainstream religious and civic organizations, regardless of the relative evidentiary strength of either side.

4. From Narrative to Policy

The "civilizational jihad" framework did not remain confined to books and advocacy literature. It shaped concrete American law and policy across three domains.

4.1 Travel Restrictions and the Courts

Executive Order 13769 (2017) and its successor orders — commonly described as the "Muslim ban" — restricted entry from a list of majority-Muslim countries. Lower federal courts repeatedly enjoined earlier versions on the grounds of demonstrated religious animus (International Refugee Assistance Project v. Trump, 2017). The U.S. Supreme Court ultimately upheld a narrowed version of the policy in Trump v. Hawaii (2018), but Justice Sotomayor's dissent specifically cited the President's own public statements, including campaign-era promises of a "Muslim ban," as evidence that the policy's stated national-security rationale was a pretext for religious discrimination (Trump v. Hawaii, 2018).

4.2 State "Anti-Sharia" Legislation

Versions of Yerushalmi's ALAC model bill were introduced in more than twenty state legislatures and enacted in several (Center for American Progress, 2011). Oklahoma's 2010 ballot measure, which explicitly named and barred consideration of "Sharia Law" in state courts, was struck down as unconstitutional in Awad v. Ziriax (10th Cir., 2012); the appellate court found the measure specifically targeted a single religion's legal tradition without any demonstrated instance of Sharia law actually being applied in an Oklahoma court.

4.3 Surveillance Programs

A Pulitzer Prize–winning Associated Press investigation revealed that the New York Police Department operated a secret, years-long surveillance program mapping mosques, Muslim student associations, and businesses across the New York metropolitan area and beyond, without individualized suspicion of wrongdoing (Apuzzo & Goldman, 2011–2012). The city's own internal assessment, disclosed through subsequent litigation, found the program had generated no concrete investigative leads; the resulting lawsuit was settled with court-ordered reforms and independent oversight (Raza v. City of New York, 2017).

5. What Mainstream Scholarship and Watchdogs Find

5.1 Academic Assessments

Specialists in Islamic law, religious studies, and Muslim political organizing have consistently found the "civilizational jihad" thesis empirically unsupported as a description of organized Muslim civic life in Western democracies. John Esposito and Ibrahim Kalin's edited volume Islamophobia: The Challenge of Pluralism in the 21st Century (Esposito & Kalin, 2011) and Nathan Lean's The Islamophobia Industry (Lean, 2012) independently trace the same funding and citation network documented in Fear, Inc., concluding that the thesis functions chiefly as a political mobilising narrative rather than a finding that has survived independent empirical scrutiny.

5.2 Measured Effects: Hate Crimes and Discrimination

The effect that is independently measurable — through FBI hate-crime statistics rather than through advocacy literature on either side — is a documented rise in anti-Muslim hate crimes correlating with periods of heightened "civilizational jihad" and related rhetoric, notably after the September 11 attacks and again during the 2015–2016 U.S. election cycle (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Hate Crime Statistics, 2002–2017; Pew Research Center, 2017). The Council on American-Islamic Relations' annual civil rights reports document a parallel rise in workplace discrimination complaints, school-based bullying of Muslim children, and mosque vandalism reports during the same periods (CAIR, 2017).

None of this denies that designated terrorist organizations — al-Qaeda, the Islamic State, Boko Haram, and others — are real, lethal, and responsible for the deaths of many thousands of people, including a disproportionate number of Christians in specific regions. The distinction mainstream scholarship draws is between that documented, organizationally specific threat and the much broader claim that ordinary Muslim civic participation in pluralist democracies constitutes evidence of the same coordinated campaign. Court rulings, federal crime statistics, and peer-reviewed research support the former distinction and undermine the latter equivalence.

6. Where Christians Are Actually Under Documented Threat

This is the section in which the "civilizational jihad" framework gets something importantly right in spirit and wrong in mechanism: Christians in parts of the world do face severe, well-documented persecution. The analytical error is treating Nigeria, Western Europe, and the United States as instances of one identical phenomenon, when the evidence for each is markedly different in kind and scale.

6.1 Nigeria: Boko Haram, ISWAP, and the Middle Belt

Nigeria is consistently ranked among the most dangerous countries in the world in which to be a Christian. Open Doors' World Watch List 2024 placed Nigeria sixth globally and reported that the country accounted for the large majority of Christians killed worldwide for their faith that year (Open Doors, 2024). The U.S. Commission on International Religious Freedom has recommended Nigeria for Country of Particular Concern designation in successive reports, citing both Boko Haram/ISWAP attacks on churches and Christian communities and an inadequate state security response in affected northern and Middle Belt states (USCIRF, 2023).

The principal perpetrators here are identifiable, U.S.- and UN-designated terrorist organizations: Boko Haram and its breakaway faction ISWAP, responsible for the 2014 Chibok schoolgirls' abduction and repeated, well-documented massacres of Christian communities across Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa states (Council on Foreign Relations, ongoing). This is organized, lethal, targeted persecution, and there is no responsible reason to describe it in softer terms.

The Middle Belt farmer-herder violence is analytically distinct and more contested. Research by the International Crisis Group (2017, 2021) and Mercy Corps attributes a substantial share of this violence to competition over land and water resources, intensified by climate stress and population growth, with religious and ethnic identity — frequently Christian farming communities against Fulani Muslim herders — overlaying, rather than singularly causing, the underlying resource conflict. Treating this violence as proof of one coordinated national "civilizational jihad" plan obscures the specific policy levers — land-use governance, climate adaptation, community-level dispute resolution — that this research identifies as most likely to reduce it, and for which the conspiracy framework offers no corresponding intervention.

The expansion of criminal Sharia law across twelve northern Nigerian states since 1999 raises a separate, genuine constitutional concern about the legal status of religious minorities in those jurisdictions (Ostien, 2007). It is a domestic federalism and human-rights question documented by Nigerian and international legal scholars — not evidence linking Boko Haram's violence, Middle Belt land conflict, and unrelated debates over American university diversity programming into a single global strategy.

6.2 Europe: Isolated Violence and Church-State Friction

The European picture differs again. Documented episodes include isolated violent attacks — such as the 2020 Nice church stabbing — alongside a more persistent pattern of nonviolent friction: debates over blasphemy and secularism statutes, and periodic church vandalism tracked by national interior ministries, including several hundred recorded anti-Christian acts (predominantly vandalism) annually in France in recent years (Ministère de l'Intérieur, France, annual statistics). These incidents are real and merit serious attention from European authorities, but they are categorically different in scale, organization, and lethality from Nigeria's terrorist insurgency, and the evidence does not support describing them as a coordinated continental campaign.

6.3 United States: Culture War Rather Than Violence

In the American context, the "civilizational jihad" framework is, in practice, almost entirely about domestic culture-war politics — university curricula, corporate diversity programming, immigration policy — rather than any documented pattern of violence against American Christians by Muslim Americans. No FBI hate-crime dataset, USCIRF report, or peer-reviewed study identifies organized anti-Christian violence in the United States remotely comparable in scale or organization to what is occurring in northeastern Nigeria. What is described as "subversion" in this context is, overwhelmingly, ordinary civic participation — voting, advocacy, litigation — protected by, and dependent on, the same constitutional order American Christians rely on for their own religious-liberty protections.

7. Discussion: The Cost of Conflation

Treating Nigeria, Europe, and the United States as instances of one undifferentiated "civilizational jihad" produces two failures simultaneously, and they compound rather than offset one another.

First, it does nothing to protect Nigerian Christians. Boko Haram and ISWAP are not weakened by anti-Sharia ballot measures in Oklahoma, by surveillance of mosques in New Jersey, or by debates over American university diversity offices. The interventions that empirical research associates with reducing Nigerian violence — security-sector reform, land-governance reform, climate-adaptation investment, and direct humanitarian and advocacy support for displaced and threatened Christian communities through organizations such as Open Doors and International Christian Concern — are entirely unrelated to, and in no way advanced by, the Western institutional-capture framework examined in Sections 2–5.

Second, the framework produces a documented, measurable cost in the societies where it has been most actively promoted: rising hate crimes, employment and educational discrimination, and laws repeatedly struck down by courts as unconstitutional (Awad v. Ziriax, 2012), without any corresponding security or religious-liberty benefit demonstrated in the peer-reviewed or watchdog literature reviewed in Section 5. A framework that imposes real costs in one domain while producing no measurable benefit in the domain it claims to address — protecting Christians from actual violence — does not withstand ordinary cost-benefit scrutiny, irrespective of how genuinely felt the underlying concern for persecuted Christians may be.

8. Conclusion: Toward a Precise Christian Response

Taking persecuted Christians seriously requires precision about who is doing the persecuting. Boko Haram is a designated terrorist organization responsible for documented mass killings of Christians in northeastern Nigeria. A Middle Belt land dispute intensified by drought and population pressure is a different phenomenon, requiring different remedies. A British family-arbitration tribunal handling a voluntary civil settlement, an American university's diversity office, and a Nigerian terrorist insurgency are not the same event observed in three locations; collapsing them into a single narrative substitutes a sense of total threat for the specific analysis each situation actually requires.

The evidence reviewed in this article — court rulings, federal hate-crime statistics, peer-reviewed scholarship, and the reporting of religious-freedom watchdog organizations across the political spectrum — does not support the "civilizational jihad" thesis as a unified global explanation. It does fully support the narrower, more specific claim that Boko Haram and ISWAP are waging a sustained, lethal campaign against Nigerian Christian communities, and that this campaign deserves to be named, resourced, and addressed on its own terms rather than absorbed into a broader narrative whose principal documented effect, in the societies that have embraced it most fully, has been increased discrimination against ordinary Muslims rather than increased safety for Christians anywhere.
