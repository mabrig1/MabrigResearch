"Na Statistics We Go Chop?": Macroeconomic Gaslighting, the 'Stomach Infrastructure' Paradox, and Nigeria's Trillion-Dollar Delusion (2023–2026)

Abstract

Between 2023 and 2026, Nigeria embarked on one of the most consequential and socially disruptive macroeconomic experiments in its post-independence history. The simultaneous removal of the long-standing fuel subsidy and the unification of foreign exchange windows produced a profound and well-documented divergence between official macroeconomic triumphalism and the lived microeconomic realities of ordinary citizens. This article introduces and develops two interlocking conceptual innovations: "macroeconomic gaslighting," the institutional construction and aggressive dissemination of selective, nominal, and low-base statistical narratives that systematically obscure deteriorating household welfare; and the "stomach infrastructure paradox," the structural neglect of the foundational layer of household food security, energy access, and real purchasing power that ultimately invalidates the mathematical and political assumptions underpinning elite-driven adjustment programmes.

Drawing on secondary quantitative data from the NBS, CBN, IMF World Economic Outlook (April 2026), World Bank reports, and Debt Management Office releases, alongside qualitative discourse analysis of high-level official statements and public counter-narratives, the paper demonstrates how Nigeria's dollar-denominated nominal GDP plummeted from approximately $487 billion in 2023 to around $252 billion in 2024, relegating the country to fourth position in Africa. Simultaneously, public debt surged toward ₦155 trillion, headline inflation peaked above 33 percent, and tens of millions of additional Nigerians were pushed into multidimensional poverty. The paper concludes with sequenced policy lessons: social protection and productive investment in agriculture, security, and energy must precede or accompany macro shocks — not follow them as palliative afterthoughts.

1. Introduction: The PowerPoint Versus the Pavement

Few periods in modern African economic history so starkly illustrate the decoupling of official macroeconomic indicators from the daily struggle for survival as Nigeria's structural adjustment era of 2023–2026. On 29 May 2023, President Bola Ahmed Tinubu stood before the nation and declared the end of the fuel subsidy regime that had, for decades, consumed a substantial share of federal revenue while delivering uneven benefits. Weeks later, the Central Bank of Nigeria unified the multiple exchange rate windows that had long distorted resource allocation and fostered rent-seeking. These were textbook orthodox prescriptions—long demanded by the IMF, World Bank, and domestic technocrats—who argued that the old regime was fiscally unsustainable, regressive, and a barrier to genuine market signals (IMF, 2022; World Bank, 2023).

What followed was not the anticipated deluge of foreign direct investment and rapid convergence toward a trillion-dollar economy, but something far more complex and painful. The Naira, which had traded around ₦460 to the dollar in the months before the June 2023 unification, entered a period of sharp depreciation, eventually trading in bands between ₦1,300 and peaks above ₦1,700 before settling around ₦1,350–1,400 by mid-2026 (CBN data; Trading Economics, 2026). Because GDP for international comparison is denominated in dollars, Nigeria's nominal economy in USD terms contracted dramatically—from roughly $487 billion in 2023 to approximately $252 billion in 2024—pushing the country from Africa's largest or second-largest economy to fourth place behind South Africa, Egypt, and Algeria (World Bank, 2025; IMF WEO April 2026).

"Na statistics we go chop?"—roughly, "Will we eat statistics?"—ceased to be a mere cynical joke and became a precise diagnosis of the policy disconnect.

On the streets of Lagos, Kano, Port Harcourt, and Nsukka, a different language emerged. The colloquial Nigerian expression captured the crisis with devastating precision. While Abuja and Washington celebrated narrowed fiscal deficits, improved non-oil revenue collections, and a booming Nigerian Exchange (NGX) in nominal Naira terms, millions of households confronted a brutal cost-of-living crisis. Food inflation surged past 40 percent at its peak; the price of a litre of petrol jumped from around ₦185–200 to over ₦1,200–1,400 at various points; staple commodities such as rice, beans, garri, and yam doubled or tripled in price.

This article contends that the defining feature of Nigeria's 2023–2026 economic episode was not merely the pain of adjustment, but the sustained institutional effort to narrate that pain as triumphant success. We term this phenomenon macroeconomic gaslighting: the systematic deployment of selective metrics, low-base comparisons, and forward-looking rhetoric that constructs an alternative reality in which "the worst is over" while the material conditions of the majority deteriorate. The apotheosis arrived in December 2025 when First Lady Senator Oluremi Tinubu declared that by 2026 Nigeria would be so prosperous it would begin lending money to other countries—at a moment when Nigeria was actively negotiating fresh multi-billion-dollar facilities from the World Bank and IMF, with public debt approaching ₦155 trillion (Punch, 8 December 2025).

The paper makes three interrelated contributions. First, it provides a granular, evidence-based anatomy of three core delusions: the trillion-dollar GDP fantasy; the creditor-nation overreach; and the low-base growth braggadocio. Second, it develops the concept of stomach infrastructure as an analytical category—the irreducible material base of household food security, affordable energy, and real purchasing power without which no macroeconomic "stabilization" can be considered socially or politically sustainable. Third, it situates the Nigerian case within comparative African and global literatures on the social costs of rapid subsidy removal and exchange-rate liberalization in commodity-dependent, institutionally weak contexts.

Methodologically, this is a critical policy analysis combining descriptive quantitative analysis of official statistics with interpretive discourse analysis of elite rhetoric and popular counter-narratives. Data are drawn from publicly available series of the NBS, CBN, Debt Management Office (DMO), IMF World Economic Outlook (April 2026 vintage), World Bank Nigeria reports (2023–2025), and contemporaneous journalistic and social media archives.

2. Historical and Comparative Context: Echoes of Structural Adjustment

Nigeria's 2023 reforms did not emerge in a vacuum. They reprised, with notable differences in speed and communication, the logic of the Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP) imposed under General Ibrahim Babangida in 1986. That earlier episode, also justified by fiscal crisis and the need to "get prices right," produced severe social dislocations, a sharp rise in poverty and inequality, the collapse of domestic manufacturing, and the infamous "SAP riots" of 1988–1989 (Mkandawire & Soludo, 1999; Olukoshi, 1993). The intellectual and political backlash against SAP's social costs shaped a generation of Nigerian economists and activists who remain deeply skeptical of rapid, externally prescribed liberalization without robust domestic safety nets and productive investment.

Internationally, the comparative record on fuel subsidy removal is mixed and highly context-dependent. Successful cases—such as Indonesia's multi-year phased reforms under Yudhoyono and Jokowi, or Ghana's 2005 and subsequent adjustments—typically featured three enabling conditions largely absent in Nigeria's 2023 rollout: (1) credible, well-targeted, and adequately funded cash or in-kind transfers to the poorest quintiles before or concurrent with price shocks; (2) visible, high-profile investments in public transport or domestic refining capacity that offered citizens a tangible "new deal" narrative; and (3) broad elite and media consensus achieved through transparent negotiation rather than unilateral executive announcement (Beaton et al., 2013; IMF, 2013; Siddig et al., 2015). Where these conditions were missing—as in several North African cases or Nigeria's 2012 "Occupy Nigeria" protests—the result was often protest, partial reversal, or political erosion.

The exchange-rate unification component carried its own historical baggage. Nigeria had experimented with multiple windows, official rates, and parallel-market premiums for decades, each iteration generating arbitrage rents for connected actors while punishing ordinary importers, manufacturers, and diaspora remitters. The 2023 unification was presented as the final, decisive break with this distortionary past. In practice, the initial overshoot in depreciation—far beyond many private-sector forecasts—amplified imported inflation at a moment when domestic supply chains were already reeling from the fuel price shock. The pass-through from exchange rate to domestic prices in Nigeria is notoriously high because of heavy dependence on imported refined petroleum, wheat, rice, pharmaceuticals, and intermediate inputs (CBN, various; World Bank, 2024). Thus, two major price shocks landed simultaneously on a population with limited savings buffers and no meaningful wage indexation.

3. Conceptual Framework: Macroeconomic Gaslighting and the Stomach Infrastructure Paradox

3.1 Macroeconomic Gaslighting

Gaslighting, in its clinical and popular usage, denotes a systematic effort to make a target population doubt their own perceptions of reality. Applied to macroeconomic governance, the term captures the institutional production and amplification of statistical narratives that contradict the sensory and material evidence available to citizens in markets, transport queues, and household budgets. It is not necessarily conspiratorial; it can emerge from sincere belief among policymakers that short-term pain will yield long-term gain, combined with professional incentives to emphasize positive indicators and downplay negative ones.

What distinguishes gaslighting from ordinary optimistic forecasting is the aggressive, repeated assertion that the positive narrative is the only legitimate description of reality, coupled with the pathologization of dissent as "anti-reform," "doomsaying," or politically motivated sabotage. In the Nigerian 2023–2026 case, gaslighting manifested in several recurring rhetorical moves: (a) the celebration of percentage improvements in fiscal deficits while absolute debt service burdens and real purchasing power collapsed; (b) the invocation of "low-base" growth rates as evidence of superior performance without acknowledging that a 4 percent expansion from a deeply depressed post-shock base generates far less additional welfare than the same rate in a stable economy; (c) the forward projection of "imminent prosperity" and even creditor status on timelines that bore no relation to debt dynamics; and (d) the insistence that "the worst is over" at moments when food inflation remained elevated and staple prices had structurally repriced upward.

3.2 The Stomach Infrastructure Paradox

The existing Nigerian political lexicon already contains the evocative phrase "stomach infrastructure," coined during the 2014 Ekiti governorship election to describe the practice of distributing immediate material benefits to voters in exchange for political support (Omilusi, 2019; Stober, 2016). We repurpose and elevate the concept analytically. Stomach infrastructure, in our usage, refers to the irreducible material preconditions for human flourishing and social reproduction: reliable access to affordable food, cooking energy, transport, and basic healthcare that together constitute the daily "operating system" of ordinary life.

The paradox arises when policies justified in the name of long-run macroeconomic health systematically erode stomach infrastructure in the short-to-medium term, without credible mechanisms to restore or exceed prior levels. When fuel prices triple, transport costs explode, food supply chains seize up under insecurity and higher logistics costs, and real wages/incomes fall faster than any compensatory transfer scheme can reach the affected population, the "stomach" is hollowed out. Citizens are told to endure present sacrifice for future gain, but the sacrifice is so severe and so unevenly distributed that the political and social foundations required to sustain the reform coalition fracture. Nigeria's experience between 2023 and 2026 offers a textbook illustration of this paradox in action.

Theoretically, this framework draws on Amartya Sen's capability approach (Sen, 1999), which insists that development be judged by the expansion of real freedoms and functionings rather than by aggregate output or fiscal metrics alone; on structuralist and post-Keynesian analyses of inflation in developing countries, which emphasize supply-side and cost-push factors over simple demand-pull stories (Taylor, 1991; Kalecki, 1976); and on the political economy literature on the social embeddedness of markets and the dangers of disembedding reforms that destroy the social tissue necessary for markets to function legitimately (Polanyi, 1944; Mkandawire, 2001).

4. The Trillion-Dollar Delusion: Currency Arithmetic and the Vanishing GDP

The anchor of official economic storytelling in the early phase of the Tinubu administration was the ambitious target of scaling Nigeria's GDP to $1 trillion within a few years. The fatal analytical flaw was the failure to internalize the immediate implications of exchange-rate liberalization for dollar-denominated aggregates. GDP, when used for cross-country comparison or debt sustainability analysis, is converted to US dollars at market exchange rates. When the Naira depreciates sharply—as it did from roughly ₦460/$ in May 2023 to bands of ₦1,400–1,700+ by late 2024 and early 2025—the dollar value of the same Naira-denominated economic activity shrinks proportionally.

Nigeria did not need to experience a real contraction in physical output for its international economic size to collapse; the currency movement alone sufficed. By 2024, Nigeria's nominal GDP in current US dollars had fallen to approximately $252 billion from $487 billion the previous year (World Bank data; IMF WEO, 2026 vintage). The trillion-dollar horizon receded not because reforms failed to stimulate activity, but because the metric chosen to measure success was itself destabilized by one of the core policy instruments. The political and diplomatic consequences were immediate: Nigeria slipped from Africa's largest or second-largest economy to fourth place, behind South Africa, Egypt, and Algeria. The trillion-dollar narrative did not merely fail to materialize; its pursuit actively contributed to the statistical demotion it was meant to prevent.

5. The Creditor-Nation Fantasy and Rhetorical Overreach

As the gap between statistical narratives and household experience widened, official communication drifted from ambitious forecasting into what can only be described as administrative satire. The zenith occurred in December 2025 when First Lady Senator Oluremi Tinubu, addressing a public event, proclaimed that by 2026 Nigeria's recovery would be so complete that the nation would begin lending money to other countries. "By 2026, Nigeria will be in prosperity; we will be lending to other nations," she stated (Punch, 8 December 2025; widely reported across Nigerian social media).

This claim represented the purest distillation of macroeconomic gaslighting. At the moment of the statement, Nigeria's public debt stock had already climbed from ~₦87 trillion in mid-2023 to well over ₦140 trillion, heading toward ₦155 trillion. The country was in active discussions with the World Bank and other partners for multi-billion-dollar budget support and project financing. The debt-service-to-revenue ratio remained under severe pressure, crowding out capital and social spending (DMO reports, 2025). The notion that a nation in this position would, within months, transition to net creditor status—lending to others rather than borrowing—defied every available balance-sheet indicator.

The public response was swift and derisive. On X (formerly Twitter), in market conversations, and in comedy skits, Nigerians highlighted the irony: the same government that was returning to the Bretton Woods institutions for emergency facilities was simultaneously promising to lend to the world. The episode damaged credibility not because citizens expected perfect accuracy from political rhetoric, but because the gap between claim and context was so extreme that it signaled either profound detachment or deliberate contempt for public intelligence.

6. The Low-Base Growth Illusion and Selective Comparative Metrics

A close runner-up in the taxonomy of rhetorical distortions was the selective celebration of real GDP growth rates. In April 2026, presidential aides prominently highlighted the IMF World Economic Outlook projection of 4.1 percent real GDP growth for Nigeria in 2026, noting that this outpaced the United States (2.3 percent), United Kingdom (0.8 percent), and Germany (0.8 percent). The implication was clear: Nigeria was outperforming the advanced economies; the reforms were delivering superior results (Bwala statements and media amplification, April 2026).

This presentation relies on a classic statistical sleight-of-hand: the low-base effect. A 4.1 percent expansion in an economy that has just suffered a massive contraction in its dollar value, a hollowing out of domestic purchasing power, and a surge in multidimensional poverty generates far less incremental welfare than the same percentage growth in a stable, high-income economy operating near potential. Moreover, with Nigeria's population growing at approximately 2.4 percent annually, a 4 percent real growth rate leaves minimal room for per capita income expansion—particularly when baseline per capita nominal income had already cratered to the $1,000–1,200 range. Celebrating headline growth while ignoring per capita realities and the absolute level from which growth is measured is not analysis; it is narrative construction.

The deeper problem is the substitution of a single, easily communicable metric for a multidimensional assessment of economic health. Real GDP growth is a useful but incomplete indicator; it says nothing about distribution, sustainability, or the composition of output. When growth coexists with rising poverty, falling real wages in key sectors, and deteriorating food security, it is not evidence of successful reform but of a lopsided recovery that bypasses the majority.

7. The Stomach Infrastructure Crisis: Supply Shocks, Monetary Policy, and Household Collapse

The core analytical and policy failure of the 2023–2026 period lay in the institutional treatment of inflation and its microeconomic consequences. Quarter after quarter, the CBN and external partners declared that inflation had "crested" and that aggressive monetary tightening—pushing the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) to historic highs of 27.5 percent—would bring it down into the mid-teens. By mid-2026, headline inflation had indeed eased from its peak above 33–34 percent to around 15.9 percent in May (NBS, May 2026). Officials celebrated this as vindication. Yet the baseline cost of living had already been structurally repriced upward, and the "moderation" arrived only after millions had been priced out of adequate nutrition and other essentials.

Textbook monetary policy assumes that inflation is primarily a demand-side phenomenon amenable to interest-rate management. In Nigeria's 2023–2026 context, the dominant drivers were supply-side and cost-push: the energy shock from fuel price liberalization (which raised logistics and production costs across the economy); persistent insecurity in the agrarian Middle Belt that choked food supply to urban centers; global commodity price pass-through amplified by Naira depreciation; and the removal of implicit subsidies that had previously masked underlying cost structures. Raising the MPR to 27.5 percent could not fix broken supply chains, restore farmer security, or instantly rebuild domestic refining capacity.

The human consequences were severe and uneven. Market women, smallholder farmers, informal sector operators, and fixed-income households bore the brunt. The price of a bag of rice or a measure of garri that had been affordable on a modest income became prohibitive. Transportation costs for commuters and traders in cities like Lagos and Abuja consumed larger shares of already strained budgets. The security-food nexus proved particularly vicious: banditry, kidnapping, and herder-farmer conflicts in food-producing regions reduced output and raised risk premia, while higher fuel costs made it more expensive to move what little was produced to deficit areas. The result was a classic stagflationary trap against which conventional monetary tools had limited traction.

By declaring victory over inflation once headline numbers moderated, the state effectively announced that the demand destruction it had engineered constituted success. The stomach infrastructure of the population had been sacrificed on the altar of macroeconomic "credibility."

8. Spreadsheet Success and Street-Level Suffering: Synthesizing the Evidence

By mid-2026, a paradoxical picture had crystallized. On one register—the one visible in IMF Article IV reports, CBN quarterly bulletins, NGX trading floors, and state house briefings—Nigeria had made tangible progress on several orthodox metrics. Fiscal deficits had narrowed (as a share of GDP), non-oil revenue efforts had intensified, foreign exchange backlogs had been cleared, and investor sentiment in certain segments had improved. Real GDP growth had settled in the 3–4 percent range, with projections for 2026 at 4.1 percent.

On the parallel register of household welfare, the picture was far bleaker. An estimated additional tens of millions of Nigerians had fallen into or deeper into multidimensional poverty, with food insecurity rising sharply in both urban and rural areas. Real purchasing power for wage earners and informal operators had collapsed. Public services—already strained—faced additional pressure as state and local governments grappled with higher input costs and debt service obligations. The social fabric showed signs of stress: increased reports of malnutrition, school dropout for economic reasons, mental health burdens, and localized protests or criminality linked to economic desperation.

This divergence is not accidental; it is structural to the policy design and communicative strategy. When reforms are sequenced such that price shocks land first and compensatory or productive investments arrive late or incompletely, the immediate losers are the poor and near-poor. When official narratives then celebrate the macro "gains" while minimizing or denying the micro losses, the result is not merely hardship but a profound crisis of legitimacy. The phrase "Na statistics we go chop?" captures this crisis with devastating precision.

9. Discussion: Theoretical and Policy Implications

The Nigerian experience of 2023–2026 carries lessons that extend beyond its borders. For heterodox and structuralist economists, it confirms long-standing warnings about the dangers of rapid liberalization in economies with weak productive structures, high import dependence, and limited social safety nets. For orthodox reformers, it illustrates the political and social costs of neglecting compensation and sequencing—costs that can ultimately undermine the sustainability of the reform itself. For political scientists and communication scholars, it offers a case study in how states manage (or mismanage) the narrative politics of painful adjustment in the age of social media and widespread information access.

Three policy implications stand out. First, stomach infrastructure must be treated as a first-order constraint and objective, not a residual or afterthought. This means designing social protection systems that are scalable, targeted, and funded from the fiscal space created by reform before the shocks fully land. Cash transfers, school feeding expansions, transport subsidies for essential workers, and agricultural input support are not "palliatives" to be announced after the fact; they are integral components of a politically viable reform package. Second, communication strategy matters as much as technical design. Over-promising timelines, celebrating metrics that citizens cannot feel in their wallets, and pathologizing legitimate grievance erode trust and make future reform coalitions harder to assemble. Third, productive investment in domestic supply capacity—especially in food systems, energy, and security—must accompany or precede demand-side and price reforms.

Theoretically, the case underscores the limits of GDP-centric or fiscal-consolidation-centric evaluation frameworks. A nation can post improving macro indicators while its citizens experience deepening precarity; such an outcome should be coded as policy failure, not success with regrettable side effects. Multidimensional wellbeing metrics, distributional analysis, and real-time household surveys must become central to policy assessment, not supplementary add-ons. The "stomach infrastructure" lens offers one accessible way to keep this distributional and welfare dimension front and center in both analysis and public debate.

10. Conclusion: From Spreadsheet Optimism to Stomach-Centered Realism

The period between 2023 and 2026 will likely be remembered in Nigerian economic history as the era of "Statistical Success versus Stomach Reality." The institutionalization of parallel narratives—one glowing in PowerPoint decks and IMF boardrooms, the other grinding in markets, kitchens, and commuter buses—represents a profound failure of policy imagination and communicative ethics. The trillion-dollar delusion, the creditor-nation fantasy, and the low-base growth illusion were not harmless rhetorical excesses; they actively distorted resource allocation, eroded public trust, and made it harder to build the broad social coalition required for difficult but necessary reforms to succeed.

Nigeria's citizens do not live their lives in nominal Naira GDP figures or percentage deficit reductions. They live them in the price of garri and rice, the cost of a bus ride or generator fuel, the ability to send children to school without fear of hunger, and the dignity of work that pays a living wage. When macroeconomic policy treats these realities as secondary or as unfortunate but necessary collateral damage, it does not stabilize the economy; it destabilizes the society on which any economy ultimately rests.

Moving forward, Nigeria—and other African economies contemplating similar paths—must invert the sequencing logic that has dominated recent decades. Stomach infrastructure first: protect and expand the material base of popular welfare through targeted transfers, agricultural revival, security restoration, and energy access. Macro stabilization and structural reforms second, but designed and communicated in ways that build rather than burn political capital. And throughout, a commitment to telling the truth about trade-offs, timelines, and distributional consequences—not because honesty is easy, but because the alternative is the slow-motion delegitimization of the very institutions on which sustainable development depends.

No nation has ever successfully inflated, devalued, or narrated its way into shared prosperity. The data, the streets, and the stomach all tell the same story. It is past time for policy to listen.
