Hepatoprotective and Metabolic Effects of Bambusa vulgaris and Mangifera indica Leaf Extracts in MASLD and Diabetes: A Preliminary Investigation and Secondary Data Synthesis Across Biochemical, Histological, and Molecular Endpoints

Abstract

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and type 2 diabetes are rising sharply across sub-Saharan Africa, and two independently studied African and South Asian medicinal leaf extracts — Bambusa vulgaris (common bamboo) and Mangifera indica (mango) — each carry a documented evidentiary record relevant to both conditions. A 2026 in vivo and in silico study by Sarkar and Das reported that B. vulgaris leaf extract protected high-fat-diet rats against MASLD principally through AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) downregulation; an older but substantial literature on mangiferin-rich M. indica leaf extract documents antidiabetic, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective effects in diabetic and toxin-injury rodent models. This preliminary investigation takes those two literatures and re-organises them, endpoint by endpoint, around the precise set of biochemical, histological, and molecular outcomes that a combined hepatoprotective-and-metabolic efficacy study would be expected to report: hepatic biomarkers (ALT, AST, ALP), lipid profile (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C), glycemic control (fasting blood glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR), liver and adipose histopathology, the molecular AMPK/PPAR-γ axis, and the documented phytochemical composition underlying these effects.

A methodological note governs everything that follows: this is a preliminary investigation built on secondary data synthesis, not a new high-fat-diet or streptozotocin-induced animal study. No new rats were dosed, no new liver enzyme assay, lipid panel, oral glucose tolerance test, histological grading, Western blot, or GC-MS run was performed by the author. Every claim made about a specific endpoint is attributed to, and bounded by, what the cited secondary literature on B. vulgaris and M. indica already documents, using a deliberately conservative qualitative vocabulary rather than invented numerical values. Where the existing literature does not yet speak to a given endpoint for the combination, this is stated explicitly, and a staged roadmap toward the combined animal study that alone could supply genuinely new primary data is set out in full.

1. Introduction: Reading the Documented Evidence Endpoint by Endpoint

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) — the diagnosis that replaced non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) under the 2023 multisociety nomenclature revision (Rinella et al., 2023) — now affects an estimated quarter to a third of adults worldwide (Younossi et al., 2016, 2018), frequently alongside, or progressing toward, type 2 diabetes. The two conditions share a common metabolic root — insulin resistance, ectopic lipid accumulation, chronic low-grade inflammation — and are increasingly treated in the literature as a single intertwined disease process rather than two separate diagnoses (Friedman, Neuschwander-Tetri, Rinella, & Sanyal, 2018). Across sub-Saharan Africa, where nutrition transition and rising obesity are accelerating the prevalence of both, the case for affordable, locally available, plant-derived interventions is not a matter of taste but of access: the pharmacological agents with the strongest trial evidence, such as the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide, remain priced out of reach for most patients in the region (Newsome et al., 2021; Paik et al., 2020).

Two plants carry an unusually well-documented evidentiary record against exactly this disease cluster. A 2026 study by Sarkar and Das reported that daily oral Bambusa vulgaris leaf extract protected high-fat-diet rats against MASLD, with a published mechanistic resolution that is genuinely unusual for the ethnopharmacological literature: a clear, reported increase in phosphorylated AMPK and a clear, reported decrease in PPAR-γ expression in liver and adipose tissue, alongside improved liver histology and an in silico docking component implicating specific identified flavonoids (Sarkar & Das, 2026). Independently, a substantial body of published work on Mangifera indica leaf extract and its principal xanthone, mangiferin, documents antidiabetic, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective activity across several diabetic and toxin-induced liver-injury rodent models, with reported improvements in glycemic parameters, lipid profile, and liver histology (Muruganandan, Srinivasan, Gupta, Gupta, & Lal, 2005; Nadella et al., 2016; Jariyapongskul et al., 2025).

This journal has already published a synthesis that reads these two literatures together to propose a novel quadriherbal formulation, with B. vulgaris and M. indica as its core two components and two further African herbs left as candidates for future selection (Korie, 2026a). That earlier article asked a formulation question: what should be combined, and in what proportion. This article asks a different, narrower, and in some ways more basic question, left largely implicit in that earlier synthesis: endpoint by endpoint — hepatic biomarkers, lipid profile, glycemic control, histopathology, the molecular AMPK/PPAR-γ axis, and the phytochemical record — exactly how much does the existing published literature on these two plants already document, and where, specifically, does that documentation run out? Answering that question carefully is the precondition for designing any combined validation study, whether or not a third or fourth herb is eventually added.

The motivation for organising the synthesis this way, rather than around a formulation, is practical. A researcher designing a combined B. vulgaris–M. indica animal study — whether a high-fat-diet MASLD model, a streptozotocin-induced diabetic model, or a combined HFD-and-STZ model — needs to know, endpoint by endpoint, which outcome measures already have strong single-plant precedent and which would represent a genuinely novel measurement for this combination. Liver enzymes, lipid panels, fasting glucose and HOMA-IR, histological grading, Western blot of the AMPK/PPAR-γ axis, and GC-MS-based phytochemical profiling are the standard outcome categories such a study would report; this article synthesises what is already known, category by category, against exactly that template.

Five tasks organise what follows. First, the documented dataset behind each plant individually is summarised. Second, the secondary sources and the endpoint-category framework used to organise the synthesis are specified, together with a complete protocol for a future combined validation study. Third, the synthesis itself is presented, endpoint by endpoint. Fourth, the pattern that emerges — where documentation is strong and where it is thin — is discussed plainly. Fifth, a staged roadmap from this preliminary synthesis to a genuinely informative combined animal study is set out.

2. Background

2.1 MASLD and Diabetes as Intertwined Metabolic Disorders

MASLD is now diagnosed by the presence of hepatic steatosis together with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, reflecting consensus recognition that the disease is a hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction rather than an isolated liver complaint (Rinella et al., 2023; Eslam et al., 2020). The same insulin resistance and lipotoxic environment that drives hepatic steatosis is also the proximate driver of type 2 diabetes, and the two conditions co-occur often enough in clinical populations that interventions addressing one are routinely evaluated for effects on the other (Friedman et al., 2018). Any candidate plant extract with documented activity on the AMPK/PPAR-γ axis, the incretin axis, or systemic oxidative stress is therefore plausibly relevant to both conditions at once, which is precisely the documented profile that makes B. vulgaris and M. indica worth reading together.

2.2 Bambusa vulgaris: The Documented Hepatoprotective and Metabolic Dataset

The published record on B. vulgaris leaf extract in MASLD rests substantially on the 2026 Sarkar and Das study, which reported, in high-fat-diet rats given daily oral extract for four weeks: attenuated body-weight gain relative to untreated controls; improved liver histology with reduced steatosis; increased phosphorylated, activated AMPK and decreased PPAR-γ expression in liver and adipose tissue; and an in silico docking component implicating the C-glycosylflavones isoorientin and vitexin as plausible contributors to the observed effects (Sarkar & Das, 2026). This is a single primary source rather than a converging multi-study literature, a limitation discussed explicitly in Section 5.3, but its mechanistic resolution — a named, plausible molecular pathway rather than a generic "hepatoprotective" label — is unusual enough in this literature to anchor the synthesis that follows.

2.3 Mangifera indica: The Documented Antidiabetic and Hepatoprotective Dataset

The M. indica literature is older and broader, spanning multiple independent research groups. Mangiferin and standardized mango leaf extract have been reported to reduce hyperglycemia and improve atherogenic lipid indices in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats (Muruganandan et al., 2005); to improve liver function markers in extract-treated animals (Nadella et al., 2016); and, in more recent work, to stimulate GLP-1 secretion via MAPK- and Wnt-linked signalling in enteroendocrine cells, improving glycaemic control and insulin sensitivity in diabetic rodent models while also exerting antioxidant and hepatoprotective effects (Jariyapongskul et al., 2025). Taken together, this is a more extensive and more consistently reported dataset than the single-study B. vulgaris record, but it documents a different primary mechanism — incretin enhancement and antioxidant defence — rather than the AMPK/PPAR-γ axis foregrounded in the bamboo literature.

2.4 Why an Endpoint-Organised Synthesis, Distinct from Formulation Design

The earlier MJIR synthesis of this pairing was organised around a proposed quadriherbal product: which plants, in what proportion, for what purpose (Korie, 2026a). That framing is useful for product development but tends to compress the underlying endpoint-level evidence into a single mechanistic narrative. This article instead holds the two plants apart and re-reads their documented findings against the specific outcome categories — hepatic biomarkers, lipid profile, glycemic markers, histopathology, molecular markers, and phytochemical identification — that any combined validation study, with or without additional herbs, would need to report. This is the more basic evidentiary accounting that a formulation proposal presupposes but does not itself spell out in full.

3. Methods: Secondary Sources, Endpoint Framework, and a Protocol for Future Validation

No animals were dosed, no human participants were recruited, and no new biochemical, histological, molecular, or phytochemical assay of any kind was run in the course of this work. Every claim below originates in already-published, peer-reviewed literature on B. vulgaris or M. indica. The contribution made here is the endpoint-by-endpoint organisation of that literature and the specification of a protocol for the combined study it implies.

3.1 Sources Consulted

The anchor source for B. vulgaris is the 2026 Sarkar and Das study (PMID: 41727251), from which dosing regimen (200–400 mg/kg orally, daily, four weeks), animal model details, and the full set of reported biochemical, histological, and molecular outcomes were extracted. The M. indica literature comprises Muruganandan et al. (2005), Nadella et al. (2016), and Jariyapongskul et al. (2025), supplemented by the broader MASLD and diabetes literature used to contextualise disease mechanism and burden (Rinella et al., 2023; Eslam et al., 2020; Friedman et al., 2018; Younossi et al., 2016, 2018; Newsome et al., 2021; Paik et al., 2020). A companion MJIR article proposing a quadriherbal formulation built from the same two anchor plants is cited where relevant for cross-reference, but its formulation-design content is not repeated here (Korie, 2026a).

3.2 Endpoint Categories Used to Organise the Synthesis

Six endpoint categories, corresponding to the outcome measures a combined hepatoprotective-and-metabolic efficacy study would standardly report, structure Section 4: hepatic biomarkers (ALT, AST, ALP); lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C); glycemic control (fasting blood glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR); histopathology (steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis grading in liver and adipose tissue); molecular markers (AMPK phosphorylation, PPAR-γ expression); and phytochemical identification (documented compound classes underlying the above effects). For each category, the synthesis states what is documented for B. vulgaris, what is documented for M. indica, and, where applicable, what is not yet documented for either.

3.3 Evidentiary Standards Applied

Claims are presented using a deliberately conservative, qualitative vocabulary — "well documented," "reported," "limited evidence reviewed" — calibrated to how directly and how consistently a given endpoint is addressed in the cited source literature, rather than to any new computation. No invented numerical value for ALT, AST, triglycerides, HOMA-IR, or any other measure is reported anywhere in Section 4, because no new assay producing such a value was performed for this article; where a source study's published outcome is described in directional terms ("reported reductions," "improved histology"), this reflects the source's own published finding rather than a number generated by the present synthesis.

3.4 A Protocol for a Future Combined HFD/STZ Validation Study

The remainder of this section specifies, as a prospective protocol rather than a description of work performed, the design a future combined-extract validation study would need.

3.4.1 Animal Model and Induction

A combined high-fat-diet (for MASLD) and, if diabetes-relevant endpoints are also sought, low-dose streptozotocin-induced (for hyperglycemia) rodent model, run alongside a normal-diet control group and single-extract comparator groups for each of B. vulgaris and M. indica individually, would be required to isolate any combination-specific effect from what each plant already documents alone.

3.4.2 Dosing and Treatment Duration

Dosing should be anchored to the regimens already reported in the source literature — approximately 200–400 mg/kg/day for B. vulgaris extract (Sarkar & Das, 2026) and comparable extract-equivalent dosing for M. indica, informed by Muruganandan et al. (2005), Nadella et al. (2016), and Jariyapongskul et al. (2025) — administered for a treatment duration of at least four weeks to allow comparison against the existing single-plant datasets.

3.4.3 Biochemical, Histological, and Molecular Endpoints

The six endpoint categories specified in Section 3.2 define the measurement plan: serum ALT, AST, and ALP; a full lipid panel (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C); fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR, alongside an oral glucose tolerance test; histological steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis scoring of liver tissue and adipocyte morphology of adipose tissue, ideally using an established scoring system such as the NAFLD Activity Score (Kleiner et al., 2005); and Western blot quantification of the p-AMPK/AMPK ratio and PPAR-γ expression in both liver and adipose tissue, with relevant downstream markers (ACC, SREBP-1c) included where feasible.

3.4.4 Phytochemical Profiling

GC-MS or HPLC profiling of the combined extract, benchmarked against the individually profiled single-plant extracts, would identify which documented bioactive compounds — isoorientin and vitexin from bamboo; mangiferin and associated flavonoids from mango — are present at comparable concentrations in the combined preparation, and would flag any unexpected compound interactions arising specifically from co-extraction or co-administration.

3.4.5 Statistical Analysis Plan

A pre-specified analysis plan comparing the combined-extract group against each single-extract group and against untreated and normal-diet controls, using appropriate one-way or two-way ANOVA with post-hoc correction for multiple comparisons across the six endpoint categories, would be needed to determine whether the combination produces additive, synergistic, or merely comparable effects relative to either plant alone — a question this secondary synthesis cannot answer by construction.

4. Results: Synthesis of Documented Evidence by Endpoint Category

Every claim in this section synthesises previously published literature; none derives from a new assay run by the author. The synthesis below is organised by the six endpoint categories specified in Section 3.2, first for B. vulgaris, then for M. indica, closing with a cross-endpoint summary table.

4.1 Hepatic Biomarkers (ALT, AST, ALP)

For B. vulgaris, Sarkar and Das (2026) reported improved liver-function status alongside the histological and molecular changes described below, consistent with a hepatoprotective effect, though the published emphasis of that study sits more heavily on histology and molecular markers than on a fully itemised enzyme panel. For M. indica, Nadella et al. (2016) specifically reported improved liver function markers in extract-treated animals relative to toxin-injured controls, the more direct hepatic-biomarker evidence of the two source literatures. Taken together, hepatic-biomarker improvement is reported for both plants individually; well documented for M. indica, reported for B. vulgaris.

4.2 Lipid Profile (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C)

Muruganandan et al. (2005) reported that mangiferin reduced atherogenic lipid indices in streptozotocin-diabetic rats, directly addressing this endpoint category for M. indica. The B. vulgaris literature documents improved lipid metabolism as a downstream consequence of AMPK activation and PPAR-γ downregulation (Sarkar & Das, 2026; Hardie, 2011; Tontonoz & Spiegelman, 2008), which is a mechanistically grounded inference rather than an independently itemised TC/TG/LDL-C/HDL-C panel in the cited source. Lipid-profile improvement is therefore well documented for M. indica and reported, via mechanism, for B. vulgaris.

4.3 Glycemic Control (FBG, Insulin, HOMA-IR)

This is the endpoint category with the strongest documentation for M. indica: Muruganandan et al. (2005) reported reduced hyperglycemia in streptozotocin-diabetic rats, and Jariyapongskul et al. (2025) reported improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity via GLP-1-related signalling in diabetic rodent models — together constituting well-documented evidence across two independent groups. B. vulgaris, by contrast, was studied in a MASLD rather than a diabetes model, and the Sarkar and Das (2026) source does not report fasting insulin or HOMA-IR directly; glycemic relevance for bamboo extract is inferred from AMPK's well-documented general role in glucose handling (Hardie, 2011; Garcia & Shaw, 2017) rather than from a directly reported HOMA-IR value. Glycemic control is therefore well documented for M. indica and only indirectly, mechanistically reported for B. vulgaris.

4.4 Histopathology (Steatosis, Inflammation, Fibrosis)

B. vulgaris has the stronger direct documentation here: Sarkar and Das (2026) reported measurably improved liver histology, with reduced hepatic steatosis, in extract-treated high-fat-diet rats. M. indica extract has been reported to preserve liver architecture in toxin-induced injury models (Nadella et al., 2016), a related but distinct histological context (acute toxin injury rather than diet-induced steatosis) from the MASLD model used for bamboo. Histopathological improvement is well documented for B. vulgaris in a MASLD-relevant model and reported for M. indica in a different (toxin-injury) histological context.

4.5 Molecular Markers (AMPK Phosphorylation, PPAR-γ Expression)

This endpoint category is, by a clear margin, where the B. vulgaris literature is strongest: Sarkar and Das (2026) reported a direct, measured increase in phosphorylated AMPK and a direct, measured decrease in PPAR-γ expression in both liver and adipose tissue, the single most mechanistically specific finding in either source literature. The M. indica literature consulted here does not report AMPK or PPAR-γ measurements directly; its documented mechanism runs instead through GLP-1/incretin signalling (Jariyapongskul et al., 2025). The AMPK/PPAR-γ axis is therefore well documented for B. vulgaris and not documented (on the sources consulted here) for M. indica — a genuine mechanistic complementarity rather than a gap in the synthesis.

4.6 Phytochemical Identification (Documented Compound Classes)

The in silico component of Sarkar and Das (2026) identified the C-glycosylflavones isoorientin and vitexin as plausible contributors to the bamboo extract's activity. The M. indica literature consistently identifies mangiferin, a C-glycosyl xanthone, as the principal documented bioactive, alongside associated flavonoids and phenolic acids (Muruganandan et al., 2005; Jariyapongskul et al., 2025). Both compound classes are well documented in the public phytochemical literature generally (PubChem, ChEMBL-indexed structures); no new GC-MS run was performed for either extract or for any combined preparation as part of this article, and no GC-MS peak data, retention time, or relative-abundance percentage is reported here for that reason.

4.7 The Pattern That Emerges

Read across Table 2, the two plants' documented strengths are complementary rather than overlapping: B. vulgaris is best documented on histopathology and the AMPK/PPAR-γ molecular axis, while M. indica is best documented on glycemic control and hepatic biomarkers. Neither plant's existing literature directly addresses the full six-category set, and no source addresses any endpoint for the two plants administered together. This complementary-but-incomplete pattern is precisely the evidentiary gap that the protocol in Section 3.4 is designed to close.

5. Discussion

5.1 What an Endpoint-Organised Synthesis Adds

Organising the existing B. vulgaris and M. indica literature by outcome category, rather than by plant or by proposed formulation, surfaces a specific, actionable gap that a more narrative synthesis can obscure: the two plants' documented strengths sit on almost entirely different endpoints, which means a combined validation study cannot simply average or extrapolate from either single-plant dataset — it would need to measure all six categories directly, in the same animals, to know whether the documented strengths of each plant actually transfer when combined. This complements, rather than duplicates, the formulation-design synthesis published earlier in this journal (Korie, 2026a), which addressed composition and rationale but did not itemise the endpoint-level evidentiary base in this much detail.

5.2 Where Documentation Is Strong Versus Where It Is Thin

The strongest single finding in either literature is the directly measured AMPK/PPAR-γ result for B. vulgaris (Sarkar & Das, 2026); the broadest, most independently replicated finding is the glycemic and lipid effect of mangiferin across multiple M. indica studies (Muruganandan et al., 2005; Jariyapongskul et al., 2025). The thinnest documentation, on the sources consulted here, is glycemic control for B. vulgaris (inferred from AMPK biology rather than directly measured) and the AMPK/PPAR-γ axis for M. indica (not addressed at all in the sources reviewed). Neither gap implies the effect is absent; it implies that the specific measurement has not yet been published for that plant, which is a different and more answerable problem.

5.3 Limitations

The B. vulgaris synthesis rests on a single primary source (Sarkar & Das, 2026); no second, independent replication of its specific AMPK/PPAR-γ findings was identified in the literature consulted, and any single-study finding carries more uncertainty than a multiply replicated one. The M. indica literature, while broader, is studied across different specific models (diabetic, toxin-injury) rather than a single consistent MASLD model, which limits direct cross-comparison with the bamboo dataset. No source addresses the two plants in combination on any endpoint, so every statement in Section 4 about the combination's plausible behaviour is an inference from single-plant data, not a documented combination effect. Publication bias toward positive rodent findings is an endemic risk in this literature and cannot be ruled out for any cited study. These limitations define, rather than undermine, the specific work the protocol in Section 3.4 is designed to do.

5.4 Staged Roadmap to a Combined Validation Study

Table 3 sets out the sequence of work that would convert this endpoint-organised secondary synthesis into genuine combined-extract evidence.

Table 3. Staged Pathway From Endpoint-Organised Synthesis to a Validated Combined-Extract Study

Stage Activity Primary Purpose Key Output
0 (this article) Endpoint-organised secondary literature synthesis Identify which outcome categories are already documented per plant, and which are not Cross-endpoint documentation summary (Table 2)
1 Phytochemical standardization and GC-MS/HPLC profiling of each extract and the combination (Section 3.4.4) Confirm marker-compound presence and concentration Validated phytochemical fingerprint
2 Combined HFD/STZ animal study with single-extract comparator arms (Sections 3.4.1–3.4.3) Measure all six endpoint categories directly, in the same animals Biochemical, histological, and molecular efficacy dataset

Stages are sequential; none can be safely skipped. This article corresponds only to Stage 0.

5.5 Relevance to Nigeria and the Wider Region

B. vulgaris and M. indica are both common, inexpensive, and widely available across Nigeria and much of West Africa, which matters as much for the affordability of any future combined extract as for the affordability of the validation research itself. An endpoint-organised synthesis of this kind is a low-cost step that any research group, including those without immediate access to animal-house or Western blot facilities, can use to prioritise which specific outcome measure to pursue first — for instance, prioritising a glycemic-control study of bamboo extract (the documentation gap identified in Section 4.3) over re-confirming an already well-documented mangiferin finding.

6. Conclusion

This preliminary investigation reorganises the documented Bambusa vulgaris and Mangifera indica literature around six endpoint categories — hepatic biomarkers, lipid profile, glycemic control, histopathology, molecular markers, and phytochemical identification — that a combined hepatoprotective-and-metabolic validation study would need to report. The synthesis finds genuine, complementary documented strength: B. vulgaris is well documented on histopathology and the AMPK/PPAR-γ molecular axis, while M. indica is well documented on glycemic control and hepatic biomarkers, with neither plant's existing literature covering the full set and no source addressing the two in combination on any endpoint.

No new animal experiment, biochemical assay, histological grading, Western blot, or GC-MS run was performed for this article, and the work makes no efficacy claim for any combined extract. What it offers is a citable accounting of exactly where the existing evidence stands, endpoint by endpoint, and a staged roadmap (Table 3) toward the combined study that alone could confirm whether these two well-documented but separately studied plants behave as expected when given together — a specific, answerable, and comparatively inexpensive next step for a Nigerian or other African research group equipped to run it.
