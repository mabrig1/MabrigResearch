State Capture, Institutional Decay, and the Limits of Sovereignty: Assessing the Risks of State Fragmentation in Nigeria

Abstract

This article examines the intersecting dynamics of state capture, institutional decay, and the erosion of sovereign authority in contemporary Nigeria, drawing on documented security failures across multiple geopolitical zones — including cases of armed actors operating in fake military uniforms, the release of suspects with recovered weapons, and credible allegations of security-force withdrawal immediately prior to attacks. Synthesising the World Bank-originated literature on state capture (Hellman, Jones, & Kaufman, 2000; David-Barrett, 2023) with classical Weberian sovereignty theory (Weber, 1919/1946) and Nigeria-specific scholarship on prebendal politics, the "two publics," and federal character (Ekeh, 1975; Joseph, 1987; Osaghae, 1998; Suberu, 2001), the article argues that Nigeria's centralised security architecture has been progressively captured by networks whose interests diverge from impartial public protection, while state governors — constitutionally designated chief security officers under the 1999 Constitution (as amended) — operate under structural constraints that leave them largely unable to discharge that mandate.

The resulting vacuum, this article contends, has accelerated institutional decay, undermined the state's Weberian monopoly on legitimate violence (Weber, 1919/1946; Tilly, 1990), and produced conditions in which fragmentation — whether through negotiated restructuring, creeping de facto regional autonomy, or violent disintegration — has moved from the realm of speculation into the realm of probability (Rotberg, 2004; Risse, 2011). Extending the analysis beyond the Akwa Ibom, Benue, Oyo, and Kwara incidents that anchor the empirical core of the paper, the discussion situates Nigeria's predicament within the wider canvas of Boko Haram/ISWAP insurgency in the Northeast (Comolli, 2015; Human Rights Watch, 2013), banditry in the Northwest (International Crisis Group, 2018, 2020), and renewed secessionist agitation in the Southeast, before turning to comparative precedents and a narrowing menu of policy pathways. The findings carry implications for Nigerian governance reform and for broader theoretical debates on the resilience and dissolution of postcolonial states.

1. Introduction: The Uniforms That Lied

Picture the scene roughly as it was first reported. Somewhere in Akwa Ibom State, a group of men dressed convincingly as soldiers — eighteen AK-47 rifles slung across their shoulders — were stopped and arrested, and handed over to the police. Then, according to the account later given by the state's own governor, the men were released and their weapons returned to them, not by accident, but on instruction from an authority he felt unable to refuse (Udom, as cited in widespread 2026 recirculations of the original account; Sahara Reporters, 2026). If this had happened once, it might be filed away as an embarrassing anomaly, the kind of story state governments quietly hope the public will forget. It did not happen once. Variations on the same plot have recurred with a regularity that should worry anyone still inclined to read Nigeria's security crisis as a series of disconnected local failures rather than a single, slowly metastasising pathology.

Attackers dressed as soldiers struck communities in Benue State within days of federal troops being withdrawn from the area (Premium Times, 2018; Amnesty International, 2018). In May 2026, gunmen wearing army fatigues moved through three schools in Oyo State's Oriire Local Government Area and walked away with dozens of children (Parallel Facts, 2026; News Central TV, 2026). Weeks later, in Kwara State, soldiers were accused — credibly enough that the military felt compelled to issue a public denial — of abandoning the community of Yashikira to bandits mid-assault and relocating to a checkpoint to extort the equivalent of pocket change from passing motorists (Sahara Reporters, 2026). None of these episodes, examined in isolation, proves a great deal. Examined together, across geography, across years, and across the imagined firewall between "the Middle Belt's problem" and "the Southwest's problem," they begin to describe something else: a security apparatus whose deployments, prosecutions, and accountability mechanisms have been shaped — gradually, unevenly, but unmistakably — to serve interests other than the impartial protection of the Nigerian public (Hellman, Jones, & Kaufman, 2000; David-Barrett, 2023).

This article calls that process state capture, following Hellman, Jones, and Kaufman's (2000) original World Bank formulation and its subsequent extension to political elites and security institutions across Africa (Grzymala-Busse, 2008; David-Barrett, 2021, 2023; EISA, 2018). It argues that capture has advanced far enough to call into serious question the constitutional fiction that Nigeria's thirty-six state governors are the "chief security officers" of their states. They hold the title; in any operationally meaningful sense, they do not hold the power the title implies (Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999, as amended; Suberu, 2001). Max Weber's enduring definition of the modern state rests on its successful claim to the monopoly of legitimate physical force within a given territory (Weber, 1919/1946). When that claim becomes contested — when non-state actors impersonate state forces with apparent impunity, when communities conclude that official protection is unreliable, and when regional leaders are structurally barred from filling the gap — the foundations of unified sovereignty begin to crack (Tilly, 1990). History offers sobering precedents, from the former Yugoslavia to Sudan and the central Sahel: states that lose effective control over violence within their borders rarely remain intact in their original form (Rotberg, 2004; Herbst, 2000).

This article contends that Nigeria has reached a critical juncture. State capture at the federal level, compounded by institutional decay that handicaps subnational authorities, has eroded the practical exercise of sovereignty. The risks of state fragmentation are no longer speculative; they are embedded in observable patterns of collusion, impunity, and abandonment that have persisted and intensified since at least 2015 (Ortom, 2018–2023; Amnesty International, 2018). They are also, this article will argue, the predictable output of inherited structural fragilities — prebendal politics, the colonial "two publics," and a federal architecture engineered as much to prevent regional military concentration as to deliver effective protection (Ekeh, 1975; Joseph, 1987; Osaghae, 1998; Mustapha, 2006).

The argument proceeds in nine further sections. Section 2 establishes a conceptual framework combining state capture literature, institutional decay scholarship, Weberian sovereignty theory, and Nigeria-specific theories of prebendalism and the "two publics." Section 3 presents detailed empirical evidence of capture and collusion in the Akwa Ibom, Benue, Oyo, and Kwara cases. Section 4 widens the empirical lens to the Northeast insurgency, Northwest banditry economy, and Southeast secessionist agitation, demonstrating that the pathology is national rather than regional. Section 5 analyses the structural handicapping of state governors. Section 6 assesses the resulting limits on sovereignty and the pathways toward fragmentation, with comparative precedents. Section 7 proposes a comparative framework for assessing fragmentation risk. Section 8 considers the narrowing window for reform. Section 9 discusses theoretical and policy implications, and Section 10 concludes.

2. Conceptual Framework: State Capture, Institutional Decay, and Sovereign Limits

2.1 State Capture in Theory and African Context

The concept of state capture gained prominence through Hellman, Jones, and Kaufman's (2000) influential World Bank study of post-communist transitions, which defined it as the efforts of firms or powerful actors to shape the formation of laws, policies, and regulations to their own advantage through illicit and non-transparent means. Subsequent scholarship broadened the concept to include political elites themselves as captors, particularly in contexts where ruling networks colonise state institutions for private or factional gain (Grzymala-Busse, 2008; Innes, 2014; David-Barrett, 2021, 2023). Jean-François Bayart's (1993) account of the postcolonial African state as an arena for the "politics of the belly" — in which access to office is pursued primarily as a route to accumulation rather than as a vehicle for public service — anticipates much of what later capture theorists would formalise in institutional terms.

In Africa, state capture has been documented in sectors ranging from natural resources and infrastructure to electoral processes and, critically, the security domain (EISA, 2018; Mbaku, 2018). What distinguishes security-sector capture from its better-known cousins in procurement or extractives is its direct assault on the state's foundational claim: the legitimate monopoly on violence. When armed groups operate with the tacit tolerance or active assistance of elements within the security apparatus — through releases of suspects, return of weapons, selective deployment, or pre-attack withdrawals — the state ceases to function as a neutral arbiter and becomes, in effect, a participant in the very predation it exists to suppress (Amnesty International, 2018; William Reno's (1998) earlier work on "warlord politics" elsewhere on the continent describes a structurally similar arrangement, in which formal sovereigns tolerate or sponsor irregular violence because doing so serves narrower factional interests).

David-Barrett (2023) emphasises that state capture is particularly corrosive when it reaches the "rules of the game" themselves — constitutions, laws, and institutional designs — rather than merely the implementation of existing rules. In Nigeria, the constitutional centralisation of security powers, combined with the practical capture of those powers by networks that benefit from or tolerate certain forms of violence, represents a textbook case of this deeper, structural form of capture.

2.2 Institutional Decay and the Erosion of Capacity

Institutional decay refers to the progressive weakening of formal rules, norms, and organisational capacity that allow a state to perform its core functions (Huntington, 1968; Levitsky & Murillo, 2009). In Nigeria, this decay is visible in the chronic inability of security institutions to investigate, prosecute, or deter high-profile armed violence, and in a constitutional design that vests governors with responsibility but denies them commensurate authority. The result is a mismatch between formal mandate and actual power that breeds cynicism, parallel structures, and, eventually, exit strategies by subnational units (Migdal, 1988, on the gap between "image" and "practice" in weak states is instructive here).

When institutions decay, they do not simply become inefficient; they lose legitimacy. Citizens who repeatedly observe the state's failure to protect them — or, worse, its apparent complicity in their victimisation — begin to withdraw consent. That withdrawal of consent is the precursor to fragmentation, as communities seek alternative sources of order and protection (Rotberg, 2004; Migdal, 1988).

2.3 Sovereignty and Its Limits in Postcolonial States

Sovereignty, in the Weberian tradition, requires not merely international recognition but the practical capacity to enforce order within defined borders. Postcolonial states have long operated under conditions of "limited statehood" (Risse, 2011), yet many retained a plausible claim to ultimate authority. When that claim is hollowed out by capture and decay, sovereignty becomes performative rather than substantive (Englebert, 2000, develops a related argument about the legitimacy deficits inherited by African states from arbitrary colonial boundary-drawing). Regions and communities begin to experience the state as an external or hostile force rather than a protective sovereign. Under such conditions, the incentives for continued loyalty to the centre diminish, while the attractions of regional self-help or outright separation increase.

These three concepts — state capture, institutional decay, and the limits of sovereignty — interlock in a reinforcing cycle. Capture undermines the impartiality required for legitimate violence. Decay erodes the organisational capacity to exercise that violence effectively. The resulting sovereignty deficit creates space for alternative claimants to authority, setting the stage for fragmentation (Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012, similarly emphasise how extractive institutions, once captured, generate self-reinforcing cycles of weak statehood). Nigeria's trajectory since 2015 illustrates this cycle with particular clarity.

2.4 Nigeria's Inherited Fragility: Prebendalism, the Two Publics, and Federal Character

Nigeria's vulnerability to capture is not a recent invention; it is layered onto structural fragilities that predate the current crisis by decades. Peter Ekeh's (1975) classic analysis of colonialism and the "two publics" in Africa argued that colonial rule bequeathed African states a moral bifurcation: a "civic public," associated with impersonal, often resented, state institutions, and a "primordial public," rooted in ethnic, religious, or communal loyalty and treated as the legitimate object of moral obligation. Resources extracted from the civic public could, in this framework, be channelled toward the primordial public without much sense of wrongdoing — a pattern Richard Joseph (1987) formalised as prebendalism: the treatment of public office as a personal or factional benefice to be exploited for the advantage of an officeholder's reference group, rather than as a public trust.

Eghosa Osaghae's (1998) history of Nigeria as a "crippled giant" traces how successive constitutional arrangements, including the very federal structure designed to manage Nigeria's ethnic and regional diversity, have repeatedly been bent toward elite accommodation rather than effective service delivery. Rotimi Suberu's (2001) study of federalism and ethnic conflict in Nigeria shows that the centralisation of policing and security after the civil war was itself a deliberate response to fears of renewed secession — a design choice intended to prevent regional military build-up that has had the unintended long-run consequence of leaving regions defenceless against non-state violence (Suberu, 2001; Mustapha, 2006, similarly documents how "federal character" principles, intended to manage inter-ethnic distributional conflict, have often entrenched rather than dissolved the patronage networks that now intersect with security-sector capture). Chinua Achebe's (1983) polemical diagnosis of Nigeria's crisis as fundamentally a failure of leadership rather than of natural endowment remains, four decades later, a strikingly durable verdict. Understood against this backdrop, the events catalogued in Sections 3 and 4 are not aberrations from an otherwise healthy constitutional order; they are the contemporary, security-sector expression of fault lines that have been visible since at least the founding of the Federation.

3. Evidence I: Impersonation, Collusion, and Abandonment in Four States

3.1 The Akwa Ibom Precedent and Its Implications

The Akwa Ibom case remains one of the most explicit public accusations ever made by a sitting Nigerian governor against his own security hierarchy. Men arrested in fake army uniforms with a significant cache of automatic weapons were released following intervention from senior police authorities; the governor's public statement that the weapons were returned to the suspects sent an unambiguous signal — certain categories of armed actor enjoyed a protection that ordinary criminals did not (Udom, as recirculated widely in 2026). This was not a minor administrative lapse. It was a demonstration of capture at work, fitting the Hellman, Jones, and Kaufman (2000) definition of state capture with uncomfortable precision: powerful actors shaping the implementation — or, in this case, the deliberate non-implementation — of the law to their advantage. That the story has been recirculated years later as shorthand for systemic failure says as much about the durability of public disillusionment as it does about the original incident (David-Barrett, 2023).

3.2 Benue State: Withdrawal, Impersonation, and Sustained Violence

In Benue State, former Governor Samuel Ortom documented repeated failures and made direct, public accusations of complicity at the federal level. Between 2015 and 2023, he claimed over 6,000 deaths from herder-farmer violence in his state alone (Ortom, 2018–2023). Amnesty International's 2018 report Harvest of Death documented security failures across the Middle Belt, including cases in which police were withdrawn days before attacks and soldiers withdrew shortly before villages were overrun (Amnesty International, 2018; see also Egwu, 2015, on the deeper political economy of Middle Belt insecurity). One particularly stark incident occurred in 2018: two weeks after federal troops were withdrawn from Benue, attackers clad in military fatigues struck communities and killed at least ten people (Premium Times, 2018). The timing and the attire suggested more than coincidence; they suggested a pattern of vulnerability either deliberately or negligently created.

Ortom's repeated public statements — that the federal government was protecting "killer herdsmen," that the Inspector-General of Police was complicit, and that the situation risked dragging Nigeria into civil war — were not mere political theatre. They were the desperate warnings of a governor who understood, better than most, that his constitutional responsibility far outstripped his actual authority (Ortom, 2018–2023; Suberu, 2001).

3.3 Oyo State, 2026: The Spread of Impersonation Tactics

The pattern continued into 2026. In Oyo State in May, gunmen wearing military uniforms attacked three schools in Oriire Local Government Area, abducting dozens of students and teachers. Reports described the assailants moving with coordination, using the uniforms specifically to reduce community resistance (Parallel Facts, 2026; News Central TV, 2026). This was not a random crime; it was the professional weaponisation of state symbolism to paralyse resistance. Public protests and activist mobilisation, including campaigns led by the social-media figure VeryDarkMan, followed swiftly, underscoring how thin public confidence in state protection had become. The Oyo incident matters because it demonstrates the geographic spread of impersonation tactics beyond the traditional conflict zones of the Middle Belt and North-West into the South-West. When schoolchildren can be abducted by men in military uniforms in a state not previously associated with large-scale banditry, the implication is unavoidable: the tactic has been normalised, and the state's symbolic authority has been thoroughly compromised (Englebert, 2000).

3.4 Kwara State, 2026: Abandonment During Active Assault

In Kwara State the same month, bandits attacked the Yashikira community in Baruten Local Government Area, burning parts of the Emir's palace and abducting residents. Allegations surfaced — subsequently disputed by the military — that soldiers abandoned their positions during the assault and relocated to a checkpoint where they allegedly extorted motorists (Sahara Reporters, 2026). Whether fully substantiated or not, the mere circulation of such claims reflects a profound breakdown in public trust. When communities believe that the army will abandon them mid-attack, the state's claim to protective sovereignty has already collapsed in their eyes — regardless of what an after-the-fact military statement says (Tilly, 1990).

These four incidents share structural features that point toward capture rather than mere incompetence. They involve impersonation of state forces, significant weaponry, and either non-response or active facilitation by elements of the official security architecture. They occur across different geopolitical zones, suggesting the problem is systemic rather than localised. Most critically, they persist despite public exposure by governors, civil society, and international observers. The absence of decisive, transparent accountability mechanisms points toward capture rather than simple failure (Mbaku, 2018; EISA, 2018).

4. Evidence II: The Wider Battlefield — Northeast, Northwest, and Southeast

A focus on four widely reported incidents risks understating the scale of the problem. Placed alongside Nigeria's three other major theatres of internal insecurity, the Akwa Ibom, Benue, Oyo, and Kwara cases look less like outliers and more like a representative sample of a national pattern.

4.1 The Northeast: Boko Haram, ISWAP, and the Limits of Counter-Insurgency

Since 2009, the Boko Haram insurgency and its breakaway faction, the Islamic State West Africa Province (ISWAP), have displaced millions of people across Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa States (Comolli, 2015). Human Rights Watch (2013) documented not only insurgent atrocities but also serious abuses by security forces responding to them, complicating any simple narrative of state versus insurgent. Amnesty International (2020) catalogued the toll on children in particular, from abduction to the use of minors in suicide bombings. More than fifteen years into the conflict, recurring allegations of compromised logistics, leaked troop movements, and a lucrative informal economy around the conflict — from "ghost soldier" payroll fraud to allegations of arms diversion — illustrate how a long counter-insurgency campaign can itself become a site of capture, in which certain actors benefit from the conflict's continuation rather than its resolution (David-Barrett, 2023). A war that several successive administrations have repeatedly declared "technically won" is, on the ground, still capable of producing the kind of mass-casualty attacks that periodically dominate national headlines — a discrepancy between official narrative and lived reality that echoes the security-sector capture documented in Section 3.

4.2 The Northwest: Banditry, Ungoverned Space, and the Market for Protection

Zamfara, Katsina, Niger, Kaduna, and Sokoto States have, since roughly 2018, hosted one of the world's most active kidnapping-for-ransom economies, run by loosely organised "bandit" groups operating from forest enclaves with only sporadic state presence (International Crisis Group, 2018, 2020). Several state governments have, at various points, negotiated informal "peace deals" or amnesty arrangements with bandit leaders — a pragmatic response to an underfunded and overstretched security presence, but one that effectively concedes that the state cannot, in the near term, re-establish a monopoly on violence in significant portions of its own territory (International Crisis Group, 2020; Herbst, 2000, on the historical difficulty of projecting state authority across sparsely governed African hinterlands). The result is a de facto market for protection in which communities pay levies to armed groups for safe passage or reduced predation — the textbook definition, in Weberian terms, of a locality in which sovereignty has been informally subcontracted (Weber, 1919/1946; Reno, 1998).

4.3 The Southeast: IPOB, ESN, and the Politics of Disaffection

Since around 2021, the Southeast has experienced a distinct but related pattern: attacks on police stations, INEC facilities, and security personnel attributed to the Indigenous People of Biafra (IPOB) and its armed wing, the Eastern Security Network (ESN), alongside the controversial enforcement of weekly "sit-at-home" orders that have repeatedly paralysed economic life across Imo, Anambra, Abia, Enugu, and Ebonyi States. Nigerian security agencies and regional commentators have offered sharply divergent accounts of these events, and credible verification of specific incidents is often difficult — a reporting environment that itself reflects the broader collapse of trusted, neutral information channels described throughout this article. What is less contested is the underlying grievance structure: decades of perceived political and infrastructural marginalisation of the Southeast feed directly into separatist sentiment, in a dynamic Suberu (2001) and Osaghae (1998) both trace to the unresolved settlement that ended the 1967–1970 civil war. Whatever one's assessment of IPOB's tactics, the persistence of secessionist mobilisation more than half a century after that war is itself evidence that Nigeria's federal bargain has never fully resolved the legitimacy questions that produced it.

5. Institutional Decay and the Handicapping of State Governors

5.1 Constitutional Design and Practical Powerlessness

Nigeria's 1999 Constitution (as amended) designates state governors as chief security officers of their states. In practice, they exercise almost no operational control over the police or military. The Inspector-General of Police reports to the President; major security operations require federal coordination; intelligence structures are heavily centralised. When violence erupts, governors can request assistance, issue statements, and sometimes fund local vigilantes or forest guards, but they cannot command the decisive response that sovereignty requires (Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999, as amended; Suberu, 2001).

This constitutional mismatch is not accidental. It reflects a deliberate design choice at the 1999 return to civilian rule, intended to prevent the concentration of coercive power in potentially restive regions in the aftermath of a civil war fought, in part, over exactly that question (Suberu, 2001; Osaghae, 1998). The unintended consequence has been the creation of security vacuums that armed non-state actors have filled. Governors who attempt to act decisively — whether through public criticism, as Ortom did, or through the creation of state-level security outfits such as Amotekun in the Southwest — quickly encounter the limits of their authority, and sometimes the displeasure of federal officials wary of anything resembling regional paramilitary capacity.

5.2 The Perverse Effects of Handicapped Governance

The handicapping produces several reinforcing effects. It generates a culture of blame-shifting in which federal authorities point to state-level failures while governors point to federal inaction, and citizens experience only the absence of protection. It encourages the proliferation of informal and often unaccountable security providers — vigilantes, ethnic militias, and community defence groups — whose emergence further fragments the monopoly on violence rather than restoring it (Reno, 1998). And it incentivises governors either to negotiate their own arrangements with armed actors, as has happened repeatedly in the Northwest (International Crisis Group, 2018, 2020), or to remain conspicuously silent about federal shortcomings, deepening the broader culture of impunity that this article has traced from Akwa Ibom to Kwara.

Institutional decay is visible not only in the security sector but in the broader erosion of accountability. Prosecutions for high-profile attacks remain rare; investigations frequently stall; official narratives shift between denial, minimisation, and deflection. When a state as central to Nigeria's history and demography as Benue can experience sustained violence with limited effective intervention, the message to other regions is unmistakable: the centre may not come, and when it does, it may not be on your side (Amnesty International, 2018; Ortom, 2018–2023).

5.3 The State Police Debate: Reform Deferred

The most direct institutional remedy to governors' powerlessness — the establishment of state or community police forces operating under gubernatorial command — has been debated in Nigeria's National Assembly and at successive constitutional conferences for more than two decades, without resolution. Opponents invoke legitimate fears that state police could be weaponised by governors against political opponents, or that under-resourced states would simply create thirty-six new vectors for capture rather than one (Suberu, 2001). Proponents counter that the present arrangement has already produced exactly the capture and impunity this article documents, only at a single, more consequential point of failure. The persistence of this unresolved debate, even as the human costs of the status quo mount year after year, is itself a symptom of the institutional paralysis described throughout this section: the actors with the constitutional authority to fix the mismatch are largely the same actors who benefit, in the short run, from its continuation (David-Barrett, 2023).

6. Limits of Sovereignty and the Risks of State Fragmentation

6.1 The Erosion of the Monopoly on Legitimate Violence

Sovereignty in its substantive sense requires the capacity to make and enforce rules, including the rule that only the state may legitimately employ large-scale violence. Nigeria's sovereignty has become increasingly nominal across parts of its territory. When communities in multiple states conclude that they must organise their own defence because the state will not or cannot protect them, sovereignty has already been partitioned in practice, whatever the map continues to show (Weber, 1919/1946; Tilly, 1990).

The risks of fragmentation manifest along several mutually reinforcing vectors. Demographically, internally displaced persons from conflict-affected zones have altered settlement patterns and political calculations in receiving areas. Economically, regions that perceive themselves as net contributors to a system that fails to protect them have growing incentives to question fiscal federalism and the revenue-sharing formula. Politically, movements for regional autonomy, resource control, and outright self-determination have gained renewed traction as confidence in the centre declines (Englebert, 2000; Mustapha, 2006).

When communities believe the army will abandon them mid-attack, the state's claim to protective sovereignty has already collapsed in their eyes — and a collapsed claim, repeated often enough, becomes a withdrawn one.

6.2 Historical and Comparative Precedents

Historical and comparative evidence suggests that states experiencing sustained capture and decay rarely stabilise without major restructuring. The former Yugoslavia fragmented after elite capture and institutional failure eroded the federal bargain that had held its constituent republics together (Rotberg, 2004; Tilly, 1990). Sudan split after decades of marginalisation and violence that the central state proved unwilling or unable to resolve equitably, culminating in South Sudan's 2011 secession. Ethiopia's recent conflicts, including the devastating Tigray war of 2020–2022, reflect in part the limits of a centralised model operating within a multi-ethnic federation under conditions of elite contestation (Rotberg, 2004; David-Barrett, 2023). Mali offers a still more proximate West African parallel: the 2012 collapse of effective state authority across its northern regions, precipitated by a combination of Tuareg insurgency, jihadist expansion, and a military coup, produced a de facto partition that French and regional intervention forces have only partially reversed more than a decade later (Herbst, 2000; International Crisis Group, 2020). None of these cases is identical to Nigeria's, but each illustrates the same underlying mechanism: a captured or paralysed centre, combined with subnational authorities unable to defend their own populations, generates a logic of self-help that the formal constitutional order cannot indefinitely contain.

In Nigeria, the combination of a captured centre and handicapped subnational units creates particularly acute risks. Governors cannot reliably defend their populations. The federal government appears either captured or paralysed, or both in different sectors at different times. Armed groups have demonstrated the ability to operate across state lines with sophisticated tactics, including impersonation of state forces. Under these conditions, the logic of self-help becomes compelling for communities and regions that conclude the existing compact no longer serves their survival (Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012).

6.3 Pathways to Fragmentation

Fragmentation need not take the form of immediate secession declarations. It can proceed through creeping de facto autonomy: states and regions building independent security capacities, negotiating their own arrangements with armed actors, or aligning with neighbouring states in new security pacts, as the Southwest's Amotekun initiative and various Northwest state-bandit accommodations already illustrate in embryonic form. Over time, such arrangements can harden into alternative centres of authority. The formal map may remain unchanged while the effective exercise of sovereignty fragments beneath it (Risse, 2011).

The evidence indicates that time is not on the side of the status quo. A state that has been hijacked at the centre, and whose governors have been rendered structurally impotent, cannot reform itself from within without a fundamental shift in the balance of power — and the very institutions required for correction are, in significant part, the ones already captured or disabled (David-Barrett, 2023; Migdal, 1988).

7. A Comparative Framework for Assessing Fragmentation Risk

Drawing together the comparative cases discussed in Section 6.2, it is possible to identify a recurring sequence: elite or institutional capture of the security apparatus; a widening gap between constitutional mandate and actual capacity at the subnational level; the substitution of informal, negotiated, or self-help security arrangements for the state's formal monopoly on violence; and, eventually, either negotiated restructuring or violent disintegration. What distinguishes Nigeria from the Yugoslav and Sudanese cases, at least for now, is the absence of a single, organised, militarily capable secessionist project commanding majority support within any one region. What it shares with the Malian case is the more insidious risk: not a dramatic declaration of independence, but a slow, uneven hollowing-out of effective state authority across multiple peripheries simultaneously, each responding to local incentives rather than any coordinated separatist plan (Herbst, 2000; Risse, 2011). That kind of fragmentation is harder to see coming, and correspondingly harder to reverse once normalised.

8. Pathways Forward and the Narrowing Window for Reform

Reversing these dynamics would require simultaneous action on multiple fronts: dismantling capture networks within the security sector, restoring operational authority to state governors through a genuine, carefully sequenced decentralisation of policing, and rebuilding public trust through transparent accountability for past failures. None of these steps is straightforward. Captured interests have strong incentives to resist reform. Constitutional change requires a degree of elite consensus that currently appears absent. Public trust, once eroded over years of documented failure, is not restored by communiqué (David-Barrett, 2023; EISA, 2018).

A more workable sequence might begin with smaller, verifiable steps rather than an all-or-nothing constitutional overhaul: independent, time-bound inquiries into specific incidents such as the four catalogued in Section 3, with published findings and named consequences; pilot community-policing schemes operating under joint state–federal oversight, designed to test decentralisation's risks under controlled conditions before any wholesale transfer of power; and statutory whistle-blower protections for security personnel willing to report collusion, modelled on mechanisms that have had partial success in anti-corruption enforcement elsewhere (David-Barrett, 2021). None of these measures alone would resolve the deeper structural mismatch described in Section 5, but each would generate the kind of demonstrable, incremental credibility that the wholesale promises of successive administrations have so far failed to produce.

Some analysts argue that external pressure, elite pacts, or the emergence of a reformist coalition at the centre could still alter Nigeria's trajectory. Others point to the resilience of Nigerian federalism and the deep economic interdependence that continues to bind its regions despite political tension. These arguments deserve serious consideration; Nigeria has, after all, survived a civil war and numerous earlier crises that observers at the time also described as existential. However, the weight of the evidence assembled here — persistent capture, documented collusion or withdrawal, handicapped governors, and expanding zones of contested authority — suggests that the window for purely incremental reform is closing (David-Barrett, 2023; EISA, 2018).

If current patterns continue, Nigeria faces a narrowing set of plausible futures. One involves managed constitutional restructuring that grants meaningful security and fiscal autonomy to regions while preserving a looser federation. Another involves unmanaged fragmentation, with varying degrees of violence, humanitarian crisis, and external intervention, along the lines sketched in Table 2. A third, less likely but not impossible, involves the reassertion of central authority through more authoritarian means — temporarily suppressing symptoms while deepening the underlying capture that produced them in the first place.

9. Discussion: Theoretical and Policy Implications

This article's central theoretical contribution is to connect three literatures that are too rarely read together in the Nigerian context: the World Bank-derived state capture tradition (Hellman, Jones, & Kaufman, 2000; David-Barrett, 2023), classical Weberian sovereignty theory (Weber, 1919/1946; Tilly, 1990), and Nigeria-specific theories of prebendalism, the "two publics," and federal design (Ekeh, 1975; Joseph, 1987; Suberu, 2001). Treated separately, each offers a partial diagnosis: capture theory explains why accountability mechanisms fail to bite; Weberian theory explains why a failing monopoly on violence matters so profoundly; and Nigeria-specific scholarship explains why the country's institutions were structurally predisposed to this particular failure mode. Treated together, they suggest that Nigeria's security crisis is not a deviation from its constitutional order but an outgrowth of design choices made deliberately, for understandable reasons, at the moments of independence (1960), civil war settlement (1970), and democratic transition (1999) — choices that prioritised preventing regional military concentration over guaranteeing effective local protection (Suberu, 2001; Osaghae, 1998).

For policy, the implication is that technocratic fixes aimed solely at improving police funding, training, or equipment — while not without value — are unlikely to resolve a crisis rooted in structural incentives rather than mere capacity shortfalls. Any durable reform must address the mismatch between constitutional responsibility and operational authority identified in Section 5, the accountability vacuum identified in Section 3, and the inherited prebendal incentives identified in Section 2.4 simultaneously, or risk addressing symptoms while the underlying disease progresses (Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012; Mustapha, 2006).

For comparative state theory, the Nigerian case offers a useful corrective to frameworks that treat "weak," "fragile," or "failed" statehood as a single category (Rotberg, 2004). Nigeria possesses, by African standards, a relatively sophisticated bureaucracy, a large and diversified economy, and functioning democratic institutions at the national level — and yet exhibits, simultaneously, the security-sector capture and territorial fragmentation risk more commonly associated with much weaker states. This suggests that fragmentation risk should be assessed sector by sector and region by region, rather than through a single composite national score, since capture and decay can advance unevenly across a state's institutions even as its formal democratic and economic indicators remain comparatively robust.

10. Conclusion

Nigeria stands at a threshold. State capture has compromised the security institutions that should embody sovereign authority. Institutional decay has left governors with responsibility but without power. The resulting sovereignty deficit has created conditions in which armed non-state actors operate with growing freedom and communities increasingly look to their own devices for protection.

The risks of state fragmentation are no longer theoretical. They are embedded in the daily experience of citizens who have watched men in fake uniforms carry sophisticated weapons, seen security forces withdraw before attacks, and heard their own governors admit that they must defend themselves. When a state can no longer perform the most basic function of protecting its citizens impartially, the bonds that hold it together begin to dissolve (Weber, 1919/1946; Rotberg, 2004).

The argument advanced here is not a call for disintegration. It is a recognition that disintegration becomes probable when the alternative — rebuilding a legitimate monopoly on violence under conditions of sustained capture and decay — appears structurally blocked. History does not reward states that lose the capacity to govern violence within their borders (Tilly, 1990; Herbst, 2000). Nigeria's trajectory suggests that the costs of pretending otherwise will continue to mount until the underlying realities are confronted with clarity and courage.

Whether that confrontation produces a reimagined federation or a fractured map will depend on choices made in the narrowing window that remains. A state that arms its enemies through inaction or complicity, that handicaps its own governors, and that allows its symbols to be hijacked by those who prey on its citizens has already begun to dissolve. The open question — and it is the question this article leaves with policymakers, scholars, and citizens alike — is whether that dissolution can still be arrested through deliberate, sequenced reform, or whether it will instead be settled, as in the comparative cases reviewed in Section 6.2, by events outrunning institutions.
