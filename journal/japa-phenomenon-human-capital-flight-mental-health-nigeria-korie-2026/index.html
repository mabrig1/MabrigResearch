The 'Japa' Phenomenon and Human Capital Flight in Nigeria: A Public Health Analysis of Youth Migration Intentions, Mental Health, and Implications for National Development

Abstract

Nigeria is living through a wave of outward migration that has acquired its own name: "Japa." Most of the public conversation about it has stayed inside the language of economics and politics — the naira, unemployment, insecurity, the cost of living. Far less attention has gone to what this movement means for public health and for the country's long-term human capital. This paper looks at migration intentions among young Nigerians, the mental health currents running underneath those intentions, and the wider consequences of losing skilled professionals for Nigeria's development trajectory.

Drawing on national survey data, official health-workforce records, and recent published literature, we examine how economic uncertainty, mental well-being, social media exposure, and perceptions of opportunity shape the decision to leave among Nigerians aged 18 to 35. The evidence is consistent: the desire to emigrate is widespread, and for many young people leaving has become the most realistic route to security and a livable future. The departure of doctors, nurses, teachers, and engineers is already placing measurable strain on service delivery, institutional capacity, and the country's longer-run economic prospects.

We argue that Japa needs to be read as more than a migration or economic story — it is also a public health and human capital challenge. Responding to it will require coordinated investment in mental health support, meaningful work, stronger institutions, better working conditions, and a credible reason for young people to believe in Nigeria's development prospects again. Retaining talent is not optional for a country trying to build a sustainable future; it is a precondition for one.

1. Introduction: A Word That Became a Generation's Verdict

Nigeria is in the middle of something that feels both familiar and new. Young people are leaving the country in numbers that have not been seen in recent memory. The word on the street and online is "Japa" — a Yoruba expression that means to flee or escape quickly. What began as casual slang has become the dominant way an entire generation talks about its future. It is no longer just about a few ambitious individuals chasing greener pastures abroad. It has turned into a collective movement, a quiet but powerful statement about how many young Nigerians see their prospects at home.

Recent national surveys have put hard numbers to this feeling. The 2024 LEAP Africa Japa Report found that 71 percent of young Nigerians between 18 and 35 were actively considering leaving the country. Even more striking, 85 percent said they would migrate if they had a realistic opportunity. Polling from Afrobarometer and similar organizations shows comparable patterns, with intention especially high among the educated, those living in cities, and people in the prime of their working lives. The preferred destinations have stayed remarkably steady for years — the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States continue to top the list, and many of those with firm plans hope to leave within the next year or two.

Most of the public conversation about Japa has stayed within the language of economics and politics. People point to unemployment, the collapse of the naira, rising insecurity, poor infrastructure, and a general loss of confidence in governance. These factors are real and powerful; they shape daily life for millions of young people trying to build something. But they do not tell the whole story. What often gets missed is the human and health dimension — the mental exhaustion, the slow erosion of hope, and the way constant exposure to images of "better lives" abroad reshapes what young people believe is possible for them at home. There is also a downstream effect on the systems that hold the country together, especially the health system.

The health sector has felt the impact earlier and more sharply than most other parts of the economy.

Between 2019 and 2024, thousands of Nigerian-trained doctors and nurses left for opportunities abroad. The Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria recorded peaks of nearly three thousand doctors requesting verification letters in a single year. The United Kingdom alone has taken in large numbers of Nigerian health professionals. The practical result is a doctor-to-patient ratio that has worsened further, moving even farther from the World Health Organization's recommended standard. Remaining staff are stretched thin, and the loss of experienced hands shows up in longer waits, heavier workloads, and quiet conversations in hospital corridors about who might be next to leave.

This paper approaches the Japa phenomenon through a public health lens. We are interested in migration intentions among young Nigerians, the mental health factors that travel alongside those intentions, and what the large-scale departure of skilled professionals means for the country's health systems and broader development. We draw on recent national surveys, official workforce statistics, and emerging literature to build a clearer picture. Our argument is straightforward: Japa is not only an economic or migration story. It is also a public health and human capital challenge, and understanding it this way opens up different and more useful ways of thinking about what needs to happen next.

2. Methods: Reading the Available Evidence Together

This study draws together existing high-quality data rather than collecting new primary information. We combined findings from recent national surveys, official administrative records on health workforce movement, and relevant published literature. The main sources included the LEAP Africa Japa Report (2024), Afrobarometer polling on migration attitudes, data from the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria and the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria covering 2018 to 2024, and supporting reports from the International Organization for Migration and Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics.

We focused on young people aged 18 to 35 because this group sits at the center of the current wave and represents the largest share of current and future human capital. We looked at both the scale of stated migration intention and the factors most often linked to it — economic conditions, mental well-being, social media exposure, and perceptions of future opportunity in Nigeria. Where the data allowed, we examined differences across regions, levels of education, employment status, and whether respondents worked in healthcare.

For the health workforce piece, we tracked official verification and migration numbers over recent years, with special attention to doctors and nurses. We also reviewed Nigeria's National Policy on Health Workforce Migration, introduced in 2024, to understand the current policy direction. Our approach combined straightforward descriptive summaries with a narrative synthesis that tried to connect individual decisions to larger system-level consequences, paying particular attention to mental health indicators where they appeared and to the voices of young people themselves as captured in the qualitative parts of the source reports.

3. Results: A Consistent and Sobering Picture

The picture that comes through is consistent across sources. Recent national surveys put the share of Nigerians who say they have thought seriously about leaving, or are actively planning to, somewhere between 56 and 71 percent overall — and often higher among younger adults. The LEAP Africa 2024 data stands out: 71 percent of respondents aged 18–35 were considering relocation, and 85 percent indicated they would go if a realistic opportunity appeared. More than half of those with concrete plans hoped to leave in 2024 or 2025, and the top destination countries have stayed remarkably stable over time.

What pushes these intentions is rarely a single cause. Economic hardship, unemployment or underemployment, and the daily difficulty of making ends meet sit at the top of most lists. But two other threads appear with surprising regularity. One is a deep sense of mental and emotional exhaustion — a feeling that "there is simply no future here." The other is the powerful role of social media and peer networks. Many young people describe seeing constant images of friends or acquaintances who have left and seem to be doing well. This creates both useful information and a kind of social pressure; some say openly that they do not want to be the only one left behind while everyone else moves on.

Healthcare workers emerge as a particularly mobile group. Official records show thousands of doctors requesting letters of good standing in recent years, with peaks well above two thousand in certain periods and continued high numbers into 2024. Nurses have followed a similar pattern — the United Kingdom's Nursing and Midwifery Council, for example, has recorded steady growth in the number of Nigerian-trained nurses on its register. The remaining health workforce is carrying a heavier load, and the loss of experienced staff hits hardest in places that were already underserved.

When we step back and look at the broader development picture, the implications become harder to ignore. Nigeria already works with low health worker density relative to its population. Continued high outflows make it more difficult to staff primary care facilities, maintain specialist services, or respond effectively to both everyday needs and emergencies. Progress toward universal health coverage and several Sustainable Development Goals becomes more difficult to sustain. At the same time, the loss of young, educated people across many sectors slows innovation, reduces future institutional capacity, and weakens the base on which long-term national development must rest.

One of the clearest messages running through the data and the qualitative accounts is the sense, among many young people, of being caught between two difficult options: stay and keep struggling with limited realistic hope of meaningful improvement, or leave and accept the personal cost of separation from family, community, and homeland. For a growing number, the decision feels less like chasing luxury and more like a basic calculation about security, dignity, and the chance to build a life that feels possible.

4. Discussion: A Structural Loss, Not an Ordinary Emigration

What we are witnessing is not ordinary emigration. It is a structural loss of human capital at a scale that carries real consequences for public health and national development. The connection between poor mental well-being and high migration intention points to a feedback loop that is easy to overlook when we focus only on economic numbers. Young people who feel hopeless about their future are more likely to want to leave. When large numbers of the most capable and hopeful do leave, the sense of possibility for those who remain can shrink further. This is not just a matter of sentiment — it affects productivity, institutional strength, and the willingness of the next generation to invest their energy at home.

The health system offers the clearest early signal. Nigeria cannot keep losing doctors and nurses at current rates while trying to expand access to care and meet its own stated health targets. The National Policy on Health Workforce Migration, introduced in 2024, represents a step in a more thoughtful direction, but policy documents alone will not hold people if daily working conditions remain difficult and poorly supported. Retention will require serious, sustained investment in pay, equipment, career pathways, and mental health support for health workers themselves.

At a deeper level, the Japa wave forces a difficult but necessary conversation about what kind of country Nigeria is offering its young people. The desire to leave is understandable and, for many individuals, entirely rational. The collective cost is what should concern anyone thinking seriously about national development. Every doctor who leaves represents years of public investment in training. Every young researcher or entrepreneur who leaves takes ideas and drive that could have been directed at solving Nigerian problems. The real challenge is not to blame people for wanting better lives — it is to make staying a genuinely viable and attractive option for enough talented people that the country does not slowly lose its most valuable resource: its people.

5. Limitations and What a Practical Response Looks Like

There are clear limits to what this analysis can show. We worked with stated intentions rather than confirmed departures, and intentions do not always become action. We also know less than we should about the mental health and life outcomes of those who have already left, and of the families and communities they leave behind. Even so, the direction and scale of the trend are consistent enough across sources to justify serious attention now rather than later.

The most useful responses will be practical rather than rhetorical: competitive retention packages for health workers and other key professionals, real investment in mental health services for young people, honest efforts to improve the broader environment for business and personal security, and creative ways for the diaspora to contribute without necessarily returning full-time. None of these measures will be simple or inexpensive. But the cost of continuing with half-measures is already visible in overstretched hospitals, thinning university departments, and the growing number of young people who have quietly decided their future lies elsewhere.

6. Conclusion: The Clearest Signal the Country Has

The Japa phenomenon is reshaping Nigeria's human landscape in real time. It is driven by genuine material hardship, by a widespread collapse of hope among young people, and by the strong pull of better opportunities elsewhere. The public health consequences — particularly for the health workforce and for the mental well-being of a generation — are already measurable and will grow if current patterns continue.

Nigeria still has time to respond with seriousness and imagination, but that window will not remain open forever. The young people who are thinking seriously about leaving are not the problem. They are the clearest signal the country has that something fundamental needs to change — and change with real urgency — if Nigeria is to keep and develop the human capital it needs for any future worth having.
