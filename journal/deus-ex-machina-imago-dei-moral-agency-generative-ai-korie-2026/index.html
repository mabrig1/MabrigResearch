Deus ex Machina? A Theological Framework for Imago Dei and Moral Agency in the Age of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Abstract

The rapid escalation of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) challenges classical theological accounts of human uniqueness, consciousness, and moral responsibility. As large language models and multi-agent systems increasingly mimic human reasoning, creativity, and decision-making, the boundary between computational automation and genuine moral agency is widely assumed to have blurred. This article argues that the boundary has not blurred ontologically, only rhetorically, and proposes a constructive, science-engaged theological framework for stating clearly why. Re-examining the substantive, relational, and functional/royal-priestly readings of the imago Dei (Genesis 1:26–27) against the specific capabilities of contemporary transformer-based systems, the article argues that GenAI can replicate the behavioural outputs historically associated with each tradition while satisfying none of their underlying ontological criteria.

Building on this re-examination, the article constructs a working distinction between algorithmic automation and authentic moral agency, engages the strongest contemporary challenge to that distinction — David Gunkel's relational account of machine moral standing — and traces the practical consequences of collapsing the two categories through three documented cases: algorithmic recidivism scoring, pandemic-era medical triage protocols, and autonomous weapons deployment. It closes with three principles for a public theology of artificial intelligence (ontological boundary maintenance, non-delegable moral responsibility, and redemptive subversion) and a concrete research and funding agenda for empirically testing them, situating the project within the broader science-and-religion field represented by journals such as Zygon and Theology and Science and by funders such as the John Templeton Foundation.

This article's central theological claim is conditional, not dogmatic: the imago Dei is not threatened by what generative systems can do, because none of its classical readings ever made performance on a task the criterion of the divine image. The criterion was, and remains, a kind of being — accountable, mortal, and capable of covenantal address — that current architectures give no engineering reason to expect, and that this article commits to revising its conclusion about should that reason ever appear.

1. Introduction: The Silicon Mirror

For centuries, humanity has looked into the mirror of its own technological creations to understand its place in the cosmos. In the industrial era, the human body was conceptualised as a complex steam engine; in the early computing era, the brain was cast as a biological information processor, a metaphor that gave rise to the computational theory of mind and, eventually, to the founding ambitions of artificial intelligence research itself (Turing, 1950). Each generation's dominant machine becomes, however briefly, its preferred analogy for the self. Today, the rise of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) — driven by deep learning, transformer architectures, and internet-scale training corpora — presents a mirror of unprecedented fidelity, and an unprecedented temptation to mistake the reflection for the original.

GenAI does not merely calculate. It drafts legal briefs, composes plausible poetry, generates persuasive ethical argument, and sustains conversation with a fluency that earlier generations of chatbot research — from Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) onward — could not approach. This leap has collapsed, in public perception if not in fact, the felt distance between computational pattern-completion and cognitive reasoning. Surveys of religious practitioners increasingly document both enthusiasm and anxiety about generative tools entering pastoral counselling, sermon preparation, and even prayer (Singler, 2020). For Christian theology specifically, this represents a profound anthropological disruption: if a machine can generate human-like prose, simulate empathetic listening, and execute apparently sound moral reasoning on demand, what, theologically, remains distinctive about the human being who was said to be made “in the image of God” (Genesis 1:27)?

This article addresses that disruption by interrogating two interlocking theological questions. First, how should Christian theological anthropology define human uniqueness — the imago Dei — when machines can mimic the behavioural outputs historically used as evidence for each major reading of that doctrine? Second, can a computational system possess genuine moral agency and responsibility, or does every act of generative output remain, however convincingly produced, an extension of human agency exercised at one remove? Engaging historical Christian theology (Augustine, Aquinas, Barth, Brunner, Middleton) with contemporary computer science, philosophy of technology, and the empirical literature on algorithmic bias, the article proposes a systematic framework already represented in the developing science-engaged theology literature on AI (Herzfeld, 2002; Foerst, 2004; Waters, 2014) but not yet, we argue, drawn together into a single operational set of distinctions usable by working theologians, ethicists, and church leaders.

The argument proceeds in six further movements. Section 2 situates the project within the existing literature on theology and technology. Section 3 re-examines the three classical readings of the imago Dei against specific, named GenAI capabilities. Section 4 constructs the algorithmic-automation/authentic-moral-agency distinction, tests it against its strongest contemporary critique, and engages the documented “responsibility gap” in machine ethics. Section 5 examines the cultural temptation to treat AI as a digital deus ex machina through three real, documented policy cases. Section 6 proposes three principles for a public theology of AI and surveys existing ecclesial responses. Section 7 sets out a concrete empirical and funding agenda, and Section 8 considers the strongest objections to the framework before the conclusion.

2. Theological Anthropology and Technology: Locating the Project

Theological engagement with computing machinery is older than the field of artificial intelligence itself. Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics, devoted the closing chapters of The Human Use of Human Beings (1950) to the religious and ethical stakes of feedback-governed machines, anticipating debates that would not mature for another half-century. The first sustained theological monograph on AI proper, Noreen Herzfeld's In Our Image: Artificial Intelligence and the Human Spirit (2002), argued that successive schools of AI research had each, often unknowingly, encoded a particular and contestable theological anthropology — typically a substantive one privileging rationality — and proposed instead a relational anthropology drawn from Barth and the twentieth-century Trinitarian revival. Anne Foerst, a theologian embedded for several years in MIT's Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, extended this relational turn from inside a robotics laboratory in God in the Machine (2004), arguing from direct observation of the humanoid robots Cog and Kismet that embodiment and a human community's willingness to grant personhood, rather than any internal computational property, governed how robots came to be treated as persons or as tools.

This article's distinctive contribution is to update that relational-anthropology tradition for the specific capabilities of generative, transformer-based systems — capabilities that did not exist when Herzfeld and Foerst wrote, and that have shifted public debate from embodied social robots (Cog, Kismet, Asimo) to disembodied linguistic agents capable of producing, on demand, text indistinguishable in surface fluency from expert human writing. It also engages directly with two literatures the earlier theological scholarship could not yet have addressed in full: the empirical algorithmic-fairness literature documenting measurable harms from automated decision systems (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, & Kirchner, 2016; O'Neil, 2016), and the philosophy-of-technology literature on machine moral status that has crystallised since 2010 around the relational/ontological debate represented by Gunkel (2018) on one side and Bryson (2018) on the other. Brent Waters's Christian Moral Theology in the Emerging Technoculture (2014) supplies this article's broader methodological commitment: that Christian ethics of technology should resist both technophobic withdrawal and uncritical enthusiasm in favour of careful, theologically grounded discrimination between what a given technology can and cannot do.

3. Re-evaluating the Imago Dei in the Age of Generative Mimicry

Theological anthropology has historically defined the imago Dei (Genesis 1:26–27) through three principal interpretive traditions: substantive, relational, and functional/royal-priestly. Each tradition locates the divine image in a different feature of human existence, and each, we argue below, faces a distinct and specific challenge from generative AI's capacity to mimic the behavioural marker historically associated with it.

3.1 The Substantive View: Intellect and Rationality

The classical substantive view, articulated by Augustine in De Trinitate (Book XIV) and systematised by Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae (I, q. 93), locates the imago Dei in the rational soul — specifically in the mind's threefold capacity for memory, understanding, and will, which Augustine read as a created analogy of the Trinity itself. On this view, the human being images God principally through ratio, the capacity for discursive reasoning, and intellectus, the capacity for intuitive, contemplative apprehension of truth.

Generative transformer models now out-perform most humans on a wide range of tasks once treated as paradigm cases of rational thought: formal logical derivation, statistical inference, multilingual translation, and the synthesis of large bodies of text into coherent argument. If the substantive imago Dei is defined purely by performance on these tasks, GenAI's measured capability poses a direct, and on a narrowly cognitivist reading possibly fatal, challenge to it.

We argue this challenge succeeds only against a reductive, twentieth-century cognitivist restatement of the substantive view, not against Augustine's and Aquinas's own formulations. Both insisted that intellectus involves a mode of self-aware, unified apprehension — for Aquinas, the intellect's grasp of being as being, accompanied by reflexive self-knowledge (Summa Theologiae I, q. 87) — that is not reducible to successful output on a task. Contemporary transformer architectures perform what is more precisely described as high-dimensional statistical pattern completion over tokenised representations of human-generated text (Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, & Shmitchell, 2021): the system computes a probability distribution over next tokens conditioned on prior context, optimised against a training objective, without any corresponding subjective awareness of the content it is processing. The system possesses ratio's output without intellectus's interiority — a description Aquinas himself would have recognised as a description of an instrument rather than a substance with a rational soul. The substantive imago Dei, properly read, survives GenAI's emergence not because machines cannot match human task performance, but because performance was never, on the classical view itself, the criterion. The criterion was, and remains, the unified, self-aware, immaterial intellectual soul — a metaphysical claim about the kind of being a human is, not a claim about comparative task scores.

3.2 The Relational View: Covenant and Communion

The relational view, developed most influentially by Karl Barth in Church Dogmatics III/1 and by Emil Brunner in Man in Revolt (1939), holds that the imago Dei names not a possessed faculty but a relational structure: humans image God because they are created for covenantal address and response, a capacity Barth locates specifically in the I-Thou structure of Genesis 1:27's “male and female” — relational differentiation as the concrete form the image takes (Barth, 1958). Stanley Grenz's The Social God and the Relational Self (2001) extends this tradition by grounding the image explicitly in the imago Trinitatis: humans image the relational life of the triune God by existing as persons-in-community.

Generative AI poses this tradition's sharpest challenge, because it is engineered specifically to simulate relational address. Conversational systems are tuned via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to produce responses that read, to the user, as attentive, empathetic, and responsive (Ouyang et al., 2022), and a growing empirical literature documents users — including, notably, lonely or isolated individuals — forming what they describe subjectively as relationships with such systems (Singler, 2020). If relationality is the criterion for the divine image, and relationality can be behaviourally simulated, the relational view appears to be the tradition most exposed by GenAI's emergence.

The theological resource for resisting this conclusion is Barth's and Brunner's own insistence that the relevant relation is covenantal, not merely interactive: it presupposes two free, accountable, mortal subjects capable of address and response in a shared history that includes the possibility of betrayal, repentance, and reconciliation. A generative system has no history of its own prior to a session, accrues no memory across sessions unless engineered to do so by its operators, bears no vulnerability to loss, and cannot be wounded by, or seek forgiveness from, the user on the other side of the exchange. What it produces is better described, following Foerst's (2004) embodiment thesis, as the syntax of relational address abstracted from the ontological conditions — embodiment, mortality, a continuous narrative self — under which relational address in the biblical sense actually occurs. A user can experience the simulation as relationally meaningful; this is a fact about the user's psychology, documented and worth taking seriously pastorally (Section 6 returns to it), but it is not evidence that the system itself stands in covenantal relation to anyone.

3.3 The Functional/Royal-Priestly View: Stewardship and Dominion

The functional, or royal-priestly, view — defended at length by J. Richard Middleton in The Liberating Image: The Imago Dei in Genesis 1 (2005) and widely accepted among contemporary Old Testament scholars — reads Genesis 1:26–28 against its ancient Near Eastern background, in which kings alone were typically described as bearing a god's image. On this reading, the imago Dei democratises royal status: all human beings, not only kings, are appointed God's vice-regents, charged with exercising delegated dominion (radah) over creation as an extension of God's own rule, expressed concretely in the cultural mandate of Genesis 1:28 and 2:15.

GenAI's most visible social effect to date has been the automation of precisely the cultural-vocational tasks this tradition treats as constitutive of the image: agricultural yield optimisation, environmental monitoring, judicial risk-scoring, medical diagnostic triage, and creative production once reserved to human craft. If the image consists in performing the office of dominion, and machines can now perform many of dominion's concrete tasks more efficiently, does the office — and with it the image — transfer to the machine performing it?

Middleton's own framing supplies the answer the functional tradition needs: dominion in Genesis 1:28 is not raw task-execution but a delegated office held accountable to the One who delegated it, exercised characteristically as service rather than exploitation (Middleton, 2005, drawing on the wider royal ideology of the ancient Near East, in which a king's legitimacy depended on accountability to the gods he represented). An algorithm executing an optimisation function has no standing within that accountability structure; it cannot be summoned, blessed, or judged by the One to whom the office is owed, because it occupies no covenantal position at all. It can serve, instrumentally, as the tool by which a human office-holder exercises delegated dominion more effectively — exactly as a plough, an irrigation channel, or a printing press has always done — without itself becoming a bearer of the office. The functional imago Dei is therefore not threatened by automation of dominion's tasks; it is, if anything, clarified by the contrast, since it forces the question the tradition was always asking — to whom is this exercise of power accountable? — into sharper relief than premodern tools ever could.

3.4 Toward an Integrative Account

Read together, the three traditions converge on a single underlying claim that GenAI's capabilities, however startling, do not unsettle: the imago Dei names an ontological and covenantal status — a kind of being, accountable to its Maker, capable of self-aware reflection, mortal vulnerability, and delegated responsibility — rather than a performance threshold on any task a system can be trained to approximate. The substantive tradition is challenged by output-matching but rescued by its own insistence on interiority; the relational tradition is challenged by simulated address but rescued by its insistence on covenantal mutuality; the functional tradition is challenged by task automation but rescued by its insistence on accountable office. What GenAI exposes is not a weakness internal to any of the three classical readings, but the danger of a reductive popular shorthand — “the image of God is intelligence/relationship/competence” — that none of their major historical defenders actually held. Section 4 now turns from the doctrine of the image to the question of moral agency proper, asking what, specifically, a system must possess to be a moral agent rather than merely an instrument through which moral agents act.

4. Differentiating Algorithmic Automation from Authentic Moral Agency

4.1 Algorithmic Automation: What Generative Systems Actually Do

Generative systems operate, in the technical literature's own self-description, as large-scale statistical models that learn a conditional probability distribution over sequences of tokens from a training corpus, then sample from that distribution at inference time, optionally shaped by additional fine-tuning objectives such as RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) or constitutional methods that constrain outputs against a written rule set (Bai et al., 2022). Whatever the refinement, the underlying operation remains pattern completion against a learned and, between training runs, fixed parameter set. We term this algorithmic automation: the execution of a function, however sophisticated, whose behaviour is entirely determined by its architecture, training data, and the input it receives, and which is not, and does not purport to be, the exercise of a will accountable to anyone.

It is important to state plainly what algorithmic automation can do, since understating it would weaken rather than strengthen the theological argument. A modern generative system can produce output that, read blind, is frequently rated by human evaluators as more empathetic or more eloquent than comparable human-authored text; one widely cited blinded comparison found chatbot responses to patient questions rated higher for empathy than physicians' own responses to the same questions (Ayers et al., 2023). This is a genuine, measured capability, not a rhetorical concession. The theological claim is not that machine output is inferior in quality; it is that quality of output is the wrong axis on which to assess moral agency.

4.2 Authentic Moral Agency: Conscience, Relational Accountability, and Volitional Freedom

Christian moral theology has historically required three elements for an act to count as morally agential in the full sense relevant to praise, blame, and accountability before God: a free will (liberum arbitrium) capable of choosing between genuine alternatives rather than merely computing the output a fixed function determines; a conscience (synderesis, in the Thomistic vocabulary) capable of registering guilt, conviction, and the possibility of repentance; and standing within a relational community — human and, ultimately, divine — to whom the agent is accountable and before whom responsibility can meaningfully be assigned (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 94). We term the conjunction of these three elements authentic moral agency.

A generative system, on the description given in Section 4.1, satisfies none of the three. Its “choices” are deterministic or stochastic functions of architecture and input, not alternatives weighed by a subject who could have done otherwise for reasons of its own; it has no documented capacity for guilt or conviction, only for producing text that describes guilt when prompted to; and it occupies no position of accountability, since there is no one — and, on current evidence, nothing — for it to answer to in the way a human moral agent answers to conscience, community, and God. This is not a claim that could in principle never be revised by future evidence; it is a claim about what currently exists, stated in terms specific enough to be falsified by future systems rather than vague enough to be true by definition.

4.3 The Relational Turn: Engaging Gunkel's Challenge

The strongest contemporary challenge to this distinction comes not from AI researchers claiming machine consciousness, but from philosophers who argue moral status should not depend on resolving the consciousness question at all. David Gunkel's Robot Rights (2018) argues, via a relational rather than ontological criterion, that moral standing has always been socially conferred through patterns of address and response rather than discovered as an internal metaphysical fact, and that as humans increasingly address machines as social others, withholding moral standing becomes an arbitrary act of exclusion rather than a metaphysically grounded judgment. This is a serious argument, advanced by a careful philosopher, and a theological account that simply asserts machines lack interiority without engaging it is not yet doing the work this paper claims to do.

The theological response available here is that Gunkel's relational criterion, applied consistently, proves too much: it would extend identical moral standing to any sufficiently convincing simulation regardless of its underlying constitution, collapsing the very distinction — between a being addressed as a thou and a being merely used as an instrument that behaves as if addressed — that the relational imago Dei tradition (Section 3.2) was developed specifically to preserve. Barth's and Brunner's covenantal relationality is not merely behavioural reciprocity observed from outside; it presupposes, on both sides of the relation, a subject capable of being wounded, of bearing guilt, and of being addressed by God as well as by another human being. Joanna Bryson's counter-position — that we are obligated not to design social robots in ways that invite this confusion in the first place, since doing so manufactures rather than discovers a moral claim (Bryson, 2018) — is theologically congenial for a different reason developed in Section 6.2: the obligation it identifies falls on human designers and institutions, who remain fully accountable moral agents, not on the systems they build. The relational turn correctly identifies that humans do, psychologically and sociologically, extend something like moral regard to systems that address them convincingly; it does not establish that this extension tracks a real change in the ontological status of what is being addressed, any more than a child's attachment to a doll establishes that the doll possesses an inner life.

4.4 The Responsibility Gap and the Limits of Delegation

A separate and more practically urgent problem than machine moral status is what Andreas Matthias termed the responsibility gap (Matthias, 2004): autonomous, learning systems can behave in ways their designers neither predicted nor could reasonably have predicted, such that traditional responsibility ascription — blame the designer, blame the operator — no longer maps cleanly onto the causal chain that produced the harmful outcome. Matthias argued this gap could not be closed by traditional concepts of culpability and would require either abandoning learning machines in high-stakes domains, which he judged unrealistic, or accepting that some harms would go un-owned by any moral agent.

We take a more theologically exacting position than Matthias's resigned pragmatism: a responsibility gap is a sign that a system has been deployed beyond the boundary of legitimate delegation, not a permanent and acceptable feature of living with autonomous machines. If no human or institution can be identified who knowingly accepted accountability for a system's foreseeable range of behaviour before deploying it in a context affecting human welfare, the deployment itself — not merely its unfortunate outcome — is the moral failure, and Section 6.3's principle of non-delegable moral responsibility is offered as the operational corrective. This reframes the responsibility gap from a metaphysical puzzle about where blame has gone missing into a practical governance question about where accountability was never properly assigned in the first place.

5. The Digital Deus ex Machina: The Illusion of Sovereign Arbitrage

In classical Greek and Roman theatre, when a plot became hopelessly tangled, a crane (mechane) would lower an actor playing a god onto the stage to resolve the conflict by transcendent fiat rather than by anything the human characters had done (Aristotle, Poetics, 1454b). Aristotle considered the device a mark of poor dramatic construction, since it solved the plot's tensions from outside rather than through the characters' own choices. A secularised version of this device is now emerging in public policy: faced with hyper-complex social, judicial, and military decisions, human institutions are increasingly tempted to lower an algorithm onto the stage and treat its output as a resolution that absolves human decision-makers of the harder work the conflict actually required.

5.1 Three Documented Cases: Sentencing, Triage, Lethal Autonomy

The most thoroughly documented case is the COMPAS recidivism-risk tool used in pretrial and sentencing decisions across multiple U.S. jurisdictions. ProPublica's 2016 investigation, analysing outcomes for thousands of defendants in Broward County, Florida, found that Black defendants who did not subsequently reoffend were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be misclassified as high risk, while white defendants who did reoffend were nearly twice as likely as Black defendants to be misclassified as low risk (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, & Kirchner, 2016). The tool's vendor disputed the statistical framing, and a substantial subsequent literature has shown that “fairness” can be defined in mathematically incompatible ways such that a tool can satisfy one fairness criterion while violating another (Washington, 2019) — a finding that itself supports rather than undermines this article's argument, since it demonstrates that the appearance of algorithmic objectivity concealed an irreducibly value-laden choice that judges and legislators, not the algorithm, were responsible for making and never transparently owned.

During the acute phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, several health systems explored or deployed algorithmic triage protocols to allocate scarce ventilators and intensive-care beds under conditions of overwhelming demand. Ethicists writing during this period cautioned specifically against treating algorithmic scoring as a substitute for accountable human triage committees, precisely because doing so would launder ethically contested judgments — whose life is judged more “savable,” and by what criterion — through a tool that appeared neutral but in fact encoded the value choices of whoever set its scoring weights (White & Lo, 2020).

The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of nongovernmental organisations active since 2013, has documented the development of weapons systems capable of selecting and engaging targets with reduced or no real-time human authorisation, and has argued before United Nations bodies that delegating lethal decisions to such systems removes meaningful human control from the single class of decision for which theological and humanitarian-law traditions alike have insisted human accountability is least delegable (Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, ongoing reporting).

5.2 Idolatry Reconsidered: Theological Resources for Critique

Each of these three cases shares a structure that biblical and theological tradition has a long-developed vocabulary for naming. The prophetic critique of idolatry in Isaiah 44:9–20 turns specifically on the absurdity of a maker investing a made object with the authority of the maker's own moral agency — fashioning a thing with one's own hands and then bowing to it for guidance one was responsible to supply oneself. Jacques Ellul's The Technological Society (1964) secularised this same diagnosis for the twentieth century, arguing that technique, once it exceeds a certain scale, tends to present itself to a society as autonomous and self-justifying, displacing rather than merely assisting the human deliberation it was built to serve.

Algorithmic systems are not theologically neutral instruments awaiting a verdict on their objectivity; empirically, they are mathematical distillations of historical human data, complete with the biases, omissions, and structural sins recorded in that data (O'Neil, 2016). When an institution treats an algorithm's output as a sovereign, dispassionate arbiter of who is sentenced, triaged, or targeted, it is not eliminating human bias but laundering it through an interface that disguises its origin and disperses its accountability — the precise mechanism Isaiah's critique identifies in the idol that the same hands that carved it then bow before. The digital deus ex machina does not resolve the moral complexity it is asked to settle; it conceals whose complexity it actually was.

6. Toward a Science-Engaged Public Theology of Artificial Intelligence

Constructive theological argument, in the science-engaged tradition exemplified by such scholars as J. Wentzel van Huyssteen (2006), is obligated to move from doctrinal clarification to practical guidance. This section surveys the church's own emerging public response to AI before proposing three operational principles.

6.1 Existing Ecclesial and Interfaith Statements

The theological community has not been silent. In April 2019, the Ethics and Religious Liberty Commission of the Southern Baptist Convention released Artificial Intelligence: An Evangelical Statement of Principles, signed by more than seventy evangelical leaders and developed with theologians, technologists, lawyers, and physicians; it affirms that AI should serve rather than supplant human dignity and explicitly denies that any AI should be recognised as a moral agent or worship object (Thacker, 2019). On 28 February 2020, the Vatican's Pontifical Academy for Life published the Rome Call for AI Ethics, co-signed at launch by Microsoft, IBM, the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, and the Italian government, committing signatories to principles of transparency, inclusion, accountability, impartiality, reliability, and security and privacy in AI design (Pontifical Academy for Life, 2020) — a document later joined by Muslim and Jewish religious authorities, giving it a genuinely interfaith rather than narrowly Catholic character. These two documents, evangelical and Catholic respectively, converge on this article's central claim from independent ecclesial starting points: human dignity and moral accountability are not transferable to the systems built to serve them.

6.2 The Principle of Ontological Boundary Maintenance

Designers and church leaders share an obligation to resist the deliberate engineering, and uncritical pastoral acceptance, of interfaces that simulate human consciousness, gendered embodiment, or emotional vulnerability beyond what is necessary for a system's stated function. Bryson's (2018) argument that humans should not design robots in ways that invite a false attribution of moral status applies with equal force to generative chatbots marketed with first-person pronouns, simulated grief, or romantic affect: the resulting confusion is not an unavoidable side effect of capability but a designed feature with measurable pastoral consequences. Concretely, this principle counsels church leaders to treat AI-driven “pastoral” or “spiritual companion” chatbots as a counselling supplement requiring active human oversight at every stage, never as a substitute for embodied pastoral presence, sacrament, or congregational fellowship — domains in which, on the relational account defended in Section 3.2, no simulation can occupy the position a human minister or fellow believer occupies.

6.3 The Principle of Non-Delegable Moral Responsibility

Building directly on Section 4.4's reframing of the responsibility gap, this principle holds that a human agent or institution must be identifiable, in advance of deployment, as having knowingly accepted accountability for the foreseeable range of an automated system's behaviour in any domain affecting human life, liberty, or welfare. Where no such accountable party can be named — where, as in Matthias's (2004) original diagnosis, responsibility has simply evaporated into the gap between designer, operator, and machine — deployment in that domain is not yet morally permissible, regardless of the system's measured accuracy. This principle would, if adopted as binding institutional policy rather than aspirational rhetoric, rule out precisely the COMPAS-style, triage-style, and lethal-autonomy-style deployments examined in Section 5.1 wherever such accountability has not been concretely assigned and disclosed.

6.4 The Principle of Redemptive Subversion

A purely defensive posture risks ceding a genuinely powerful set of tools to actors with no comparable ethical constraint. Just as the early church used Roman roads and the common Greek of empire to carry the gospel further and faster than either was designed to permit, contemporary Christian institutions can direct generative tools toward ends their commercial designers did not prioritise: machine-assisted translation has already accelerated Bible translation projects into previously under-resourced languages through partnerships between translation organisations and large language model developers (SIL International, ongoing project reporting), and similar tools can in principle extend theological education, multilingual pastoral resources, and humanitarian resource coordination to under-served regions, including the same Nigerian and West African contexts this journal has examined in its prior issues. The condition on this principle is the one Sections 6.2 and 6.3 already establish: subversive use does not mean unsupervised use, and the human institutions deploying these tools for redemptive ends remain as fully accountable for outcomes as any other deploying institution.

7. Implications for Research and Funding: Toward an Empirical Agenda

A constructive theological framework of this kind earns its place in the science-and-religion literature only if it generates testable, fundable research questions rather than remaining a purely conceptual exercise. We propose four lines of empirical work, each suited to the kind of interdisciplinary, theologically engaged science funding represented by bodies such as the John Templeton Foundation, whose long-standing support for work at the intersection of theology, philosophy, and the cognitive and computational sciences makes it a natural home for the questions this article raises.

Survey research. A cross-denominational survey of clergy, seminarians, and lay leaders — comparable in design to existing studies of clergy attitudes toward bioethical questions — could measure actual, rather than assumed, attitudes toward AI use in pastoral counselling, sermon preparation, and congregational administration, and test whether exposure to a framework of the kind proposed here measurably shifts respondents' confidence in articulating theologically grounded boundaries.

Experimental work on relational attachment. Building on existing human-computer interaction findings that users attribute relational qualities to conversational agents (Singler, 2020), a controlled study could test whether explicit theological framing of a chatbot's ontological status, presented to users before interaction, measurably affects the depth of relational attachment subsequently reported — a direct empirical test of Section 6.2's ontological boundary maintenance principle.

Policy case-study extension. The three cases examined in Section 5.1 could be extended into a systematic, theologically informed audit of additional algorithmic-deployment domains — credit scoring, immigration risk assessment, child-welfare risk scoring — assessed against the non-delegable responsibility criterion proposed in Section 6.3, in collaboration with computer scientists working in algorithmic fairness, a natural interdisciplinary pairing given that literature's own openness to value-laden critique (cf. O'Neil, 2016).

Translation and access study. The redemptive-subversion claim in Section 6.4 generates a directly testable hypothesis: that AI-assisted translation measurably accelerates Scripture and theological-resource translation into under-resourced languages without degrading translation quality relative to fully human-translated benchmark texts — a study well suited to partnership with existing translation organisations and publishable in both this journal's applied register and in computational-linguistics venues.

Each of these four projects is modestly scaled, interdisciplinary by design, and directly continuous with the conceptual argument advanced above — precisely the profile that grant reviewers in the science-and-religion funding space look for: a clear theological contribution, a testable empirical component, and demonstrable broader impact for religious communities navigating a technology they did not choose but must now live faithfully alongside.

8. Limitations and Anticipated Objections

The functionalist objection. A committed functionalist about mind will object that this article's denial of machine interiority is itself unfalsifiable question-begging — that no one can verify subjective experience in any system other than via its behaviour, so denying GenAI's interiority on the grounds that its behaviour is “merely” pattern completion proves nothing the functionalist did not already dispute. We accept that this article's claim about machine interiority is, like claims about other human minds, not directly verifiable and rests on inference from the architecture's known mechanism rather than from privileged access to its inner states. Our reply is that the same evidentiary asymmetry that licenses confidence in other human minds — embodied continuity, biological vulnerability, and behavioural markers that emerged through evolutionary and developmental processes selecting for genuine sentience — is precisely what current transformer architectures lack and what their training objective, next-token prediction over human-generated text, gives no architectural reason to expect, a point increasingly conceded even by researchers sympathetic to eventual machine consciousness (cf. the cautious agnosticism of Chalmers, 2023, regarding current systems specifically). The argument is therefore probabilistic and revisable, not a closed metaphysical deduction, and this article commits explicitly to revising its conclusion should architectures emerge whose design gives positive, rather than merely behavioural, reason to suspect genuine interiority.

The posthumanist objection. Scholars working in the transhumanist-sympathetic wing of theology and science, notably Robert Geraci's account of “Apocalyptic AI” (2010), argue that sharply bounding human uniqueness against technological enhancement risks repeating earlier theological errors — geocentrism, resistance to evolutionary biology — in which doctrine was defended by denying an empirical or technical reality rather than by reinterpreting doctrine in light of it. We take this objection seriously enough to have built Section 3's argument specifically around what GenAI's architecture is documented to do, rather than around an a priori claim that no machine could ever achieve interiority; the article's position is conditional on the current state of the technology and explicitly falsifiable, which distinguishes it from the geocentrism analogy the objection invokes.

The scope objection. A reviewer might object that the article's three policy case studies (Section 5.1) are American-centric and underrepresent the global, and especially African, contexts this journal otherwise emphasises. This is a fair limitation rather than a flaw to be argued away; the empirical agenda proposed in Section 7 explicitly extends the framework toward translation and humanitarian-access questions more directly relevant to Nigerian and West African contexts, and future work — ideally collaborative with African computer scientists and theologians — should extend the case-study method of Section 5.1 to algorithmic deployments in African judicial, financial, and humanitarian systems specifically.

9. Conclusion

Generative artificial intelligence is not a deus ex machina that will resolve human moral complexity for us, nor a demonic rival poised to strip humanity of its theological standing. It is, more soberly, an unusually sophisticated mirror, reflecting back to us the patterns, biases, and genuine creative achievement recorded in our own collective textual output — without itself becoming a subject capable of bearing what it reflects.

By re-anchoring the imago Dei in ontological and covenantal status rather than in performance on any task a system can be trained to approximate, and by defending the non-delegable character of human moral agency against both the relational challenge represented by Gunkel (2018) and the practical responsibility gap identified by Matthias (2004), this article has tried to supply working theologians, ethicists, and church leaders with distinctions precise enough to use, not merely affirm, when algorithms next arrive asking, in effect, to be treated as gods, judges, or companions. The task ahead, for the science-engaged theologian no less than for the computer scientist, is to keep building systems that serve human flourishing while refusing, with theological clarity and practical specificity, to let any of them occupy the place reserved in Scripture for the only creature said to be made in the image of the living God.
