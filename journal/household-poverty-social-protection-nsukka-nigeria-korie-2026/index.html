Household Poverty and Social Protection in Nsukka, Nigeria: A Secondary Data Analysis

Abstract

Poverty in rural southeastern Nigeria, particularly in Nsukka Local Government Area (LGA) of Enugu State, remains deeply entrenched despite national and state-level interventions. This secondary data analysis examines household poverty patterns using monetary and multidimensional measures and assesses the reach and effectiveness of social protection mechanisms relevant to the area. Findings indicate that Enugu State's monetary poverty headcount rate stands at approximately 58.13%, well above the national average of roughly 40.1%, with rural agrarian communities such as Nsukka experiencing heightened vulnerability arising from large household sizes, limited educational attainment, dependence on subsistence agriculture, and exposure to economic shocks. Locally grounded evidence reinforces this picture: a survey of Nsukka households found over four in five experiencing some degree of food insecurity, and a qualitative assessment of school feeding delivery in Enugu metropolis found the programme reaching schools but undermined by inadequate funding and weak infrastructure. National evidence on Nigeria's flagship cash transfer programme is more troubling still — a 2025 quasi-experimental evaluation found that beneficiary households were, on average, more food insecure than non-beneficiaries, even as the programme improved financial inclusion and asset-building.

A region-wide systematic review of 27 studies across Sub-Saharan Africa confirms that the effectiveness of both conditional and unconditional transfers is highly dependent on design, targeting, and local context rather than guaranteed by the transfer mechanism itself. Adopting a social work perspective, the study argues that community-based, rights-oriented, and hybrid (“cash-plus”) interventions — integrating formal safety nets with informal kinship support and livelihood enhancement — offer a more credible route to poverty reduction than cash transfers alone. Policy implications emphasize improved targeting, adequacy of transfer value, inter-agency coordination, and alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals in high-poverty rural contexts.

Introduction

Household poverty in Nigeria is pervasive and multidimensional, shaped by structural economic, social, and environmental factors that compound one another in rural communities. Nsukka, a semi-rural agrarian Local Government Area in Enugu State, southeastern Nigeria, exemplifies these challenges through heavy reliance on subsistence farming, limited non-farm income opportunities, and constrained access to basic services. National efforts such as the National Social Investment Programme (NSIP) — comprising the Conditional Cash Transfer (Household Uplifting Programme), N-Power, the Government Enterprise and Empowerment Programme, and the National Home-Grown School Feeding Programme — aim to provide safety nets for vulnerable households, but local effectiveness is often undermined by coverage gaps, targeting inaccuracies, irregular disbursement, and weak implementation infrastructure.

This paper conducts a secondary analysis of national, state, and locally available data to illuminate poverty dynamics in Nsukka and its surrounding Southeast Nigerian context, while evaluating the reach and effectiveness of the social protection mechanisms operating there. Beyond restating national poverty statistics, the analysis draws on recent peer-reviewed evidence specific to Nsukka and Enugu State — on household food security, school feeding delivery, and cash transfer outcomes — to ground the discussion in verifiable local findings rather than national averages alone. Through a social work lens, emphasizing empowerment, social justice, human rights, and community resilience, the paper explores pathways toward more responsive, context-specific interventions in resource-constrained rural settings.

Literature Review

Monetary and Multidimensional Poverty

National survey data places Nigeria's monetary poverty headcount at approximately 40.1% (2018/19), with rural rates significantly higher than urban ones. Enugu State records a monetary poverty rate of 58.13%, placing it among the more poverty-affected states in the Southeast despite the zone's relatively stronger national reputation for human development indicators. Multidimensional poverty — capturing deprivations in health, education, nutrition, and living standards beyond income alone — affects approximately 63% of Nigerians, or roughly 133 million people, according to the 2022 National Bureau of Statistics/UNDP Multidimensional Poverty Index. In rural areas such as Nsukka, compounding risk factors include high dependency ratios, low educational attainment, and vulnerability to agricultural and economic shocks.

Locally focused work on Nsukka itself remains limited and somewhat dated. An unpublished working paper by Ataguba, Fonta, and Ichoku, circulated through the Poverty and Economic Policy Research Network, reported multidimensional poverty headcounts in Nsukka of between 70% and 78%, driven primarily by large family size, low education, poor employment, rural location, and poor health. This estimate is widely cited in subsequent literature, but it predates the 2016 launch of the National Social Investment Programme and was never published in a peer-reviewed journal, which limits how much weight it can bear as a current empirical anchor.

More recent peer-reviewed evidence offers a partial update. A 2023 household survey of 390 women across twenty communities in Nsukka Local Government Area found that 82.6% of households experienced some degree of food insecurity, with severe food insecurity affecting 60.3% of the sample. Food security status was significantly associated with respondent age, education, work status, and income, though not with the gender of the household head or household size — a finding that complicates the assumption, common in older Nigerian poverty literature, that female household headship is automatically the strongest predictor of deprivation. A related 2025 multidimensional poverty study across five Southeast Nigerian states, using a survey of 1,057 respondents during the COVID-19 period, similarly found that educational level, marital status, and employment status were significant predictors of multidimensional poverty status, reinforcing the centrality of human capital variables over purely demographic ones.

Social Protection Programming and Its Local Reach

Social protection systems in Nigeria remain fragmented, with formal programmes providing only partial relief and informal kinship networks continuing to play a critical supplementary role, particularly in Igbo communities where communal solidarity structures are culturally embedded. Within Enugu State specifically, an early case study of the state's Conditional Cash Transfer component under the Vision 4:2020 poverty alleviation framework found that, although the social intervention policy existed and disbursed empowerment grants to extremely poor households, beneficiaries did not feel a substantial improvement in their standard of living — an early signal of the implementation gap that later national evaluations would confirm at greater scale.

A 2023 qualitative study of the National Home-Grown School Feeding Programme — one of NSIP's four pillars and directly active in Enugu metropolis, Nsukka's home state — interviewed 24 headteachers and found the programme was implemented in every sampled school, with most respondents crediting it with reducing hunger and improving attendance. However, the same study documented persistent structural weaknesses: a meal budget of only ₦70 per child per day, the complete absence of on-site kitchen facilities in any sampled school, and irregular deworming that fell outside programme guidelines. These findings indicate that even where social protection programming reaches Enugu State's communities, the adequacy and consistency of delivery, not mere presence, determines whether the intervention translates into meaningful welfare improvement.

National-level evidence on NSIP's flagship cash transfer component, the Household Uplifting Programme, raises a more fundamental concern. A 2025 quasi-experimental evaluation using propensity score matching in Akwa Ibom State — a comparable Nigerian context — found that beneficiary households spent 26.1% less on food on average than otherwise-similar non-beneficiary households, and that food insecurity was in fact higher among beneficiaries. The authors attributed this counterintuitive result to the inadequacy and irregularity of the ₦5,000 monthly transfer relative to prevailing inflation, alongside possible targeting weaknesses, while noting that the programme did succeed in expanding beneficiaries' financial inclusion, asset-building, and participation in cooperative groups. This finding is an important corrective to literature that treats cash transfer coverage figures as a proxy for poverty-reduction impact: presence of a transfer does not guarantee a positive welfare outcome, and may in some configurations coincide with worse outcomes on the very dimension it targets.

This pattern is consistent with the broader regional evidence base. A 2024 systematic review and PRISMA-guided narrative synthesis of 27 studies conducted across Sub-Saharan Africa between 2014 and 2024 found that conditional cash transfers generally outperformed unconditional transfers on specific education and health targets by incentivizing particular behaviours, while unconditional transfers offered greater flexibility and stronger mental health and resilience benefits in fragile settings. Critically, the review's overarching conclusion was that transfer effectiveness is highly context-dependent, shaped by programme design, targeting precision, and local socioeconomic conditions, rather than being an inherent property of the conditional or unconditional transfer mechanism itself. Read together, the Akwa Ibom finding and this regional synthesis suggest that Nigeria's cash transfer architecture requires re-evaluation of adequacy and targeting at least as much as it requires expansion of coverage.

A broader rural development study across three Enugu State LGAs, using a sample of 400 respondents derived through Yamane sample-size determination and analyzed with chi-square testing, identified corruption, debt burden, unemployment, and weak political will as primary drivers of persistent rural poverty, and identified endemic corruption, poor infrastructure, and lack of accountability as the principal barriers to effective Sustainable Development Goals implementation in the state's rural areas. This aligns with the implementation-gap findings from the school feeding and cash transfer literature reviewed above, suggesting that governance and delivery-system weaknesses, rather than the absence of policy frameworks, are the binding constraint on poverty reduction in Enugu State's rural LGAs.

Methodology

This study synthesizes secondary data from several categories of sources. National monetary poverty indicators are drawn from the Nigeria Living Standards Survey (NLSS) 2018/19, and multidimensional poverty indicators from the Nigeria Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) 2022, both published by the National Bureau of Statistics, with the MPI cross-referenced against the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative's statistical snapshot for Nigeria. These provide the national and state-level baseline against which Nsukka's circumstances are situated.

This baseline is supplemented with peer-reviewed, locally grounded empirical studies identified through a structured literature search across PubMed/PMC, DOAJ, Wiley, PLOS, BMC/Springer, and Taylor & Francis: a household-level food security survey conducted directly in Nsukka LGA; a five-state Southeast Nigeria multidimensional poverty study; a qualitative assessment of school feeding programme delivery in Enugu metropolis; a quasi-experimental evaluation of Nigeria's national cash transfer programme; and a Sub-Saharan Africa-wide systematic review of cash transfer effectiveness. Two further sources — an institutional case study of Enugu State's own Conditional Cash Transfer programme and a rural LGA-level study of poverty and SDG implementation in Enugu State — were retained from smaller Nigerian academic journals after their publication details, sampling methods, and indexing were independently verified; these are flagged in the discussion as carrying less methodological weight than the larger peer-reviewed sources.

Descriptive statistics and comparative analysis focus on Enugu State and Southeast Nigerian rural patterns relevant to Nsukka LGA, triangulated against the LGA- and metropolis-level primary studies identified above. Several limitations constrain this analysis. First, the data are substantially secondary in nature and span 2011 to 2025, with no single recent dataset offering granular, representative coverage of Nsukka LGA specifically; the most-cited Nsukka-specific poverty estimate (Ataguba et al.) remains an unpublished 2011 working paper rather than a peer-reviewed source, and is now over a decade old, predating NSIP entirely. Second, where studies are drawn from neighbouring states (Akwa Ibom) or the wider Southeast/Sub-Saharan region rather than Nsukka or Enugu State directly, their findings are treated as contextually informative rather than as direct measurements of Nsukka's situation. Third, the secondary and cross-sectional nature of most underlying studies limits causal inference; only the Akwa Ibom cash transfer evaluation employs a quasi-experimental design capable of supporting causal claims, and even that finding should be read as specific to one state's implementation of the programme rather than a verdict on cash transfers as a category.

Findings

Enugu State's monetary poverty rate of 58.13% substantially exceeds the national average of approximately 40.1%, and rural households in agrarian LGAs such as Nsukka are disproportionately affected within that state-level figure. Multidimensional poverty data corroborate this picture: nationally, 63% of Nigerians experience multidimensional deprivation, with health, education, nutrition, and living-standard indicators all implicated. Within Nsukka specifically, the most directly relevant recent data point is the 2023 finding that 82.6% of surveyed households experienced some degree of food insecurity, with severe insecurity affecting 60.3% — a figure consistent with, though more current than, the dated 70–78% multidimensional poverty range reported in the unpublished 2011 working paper. Across Nsukka and the broader Southeast, education, employment status, and household income consistently emerge as the strongest correlates of poverty status, a pattern confirmed independently in both the Nsukka food security survey and the five-state COVID-era multidimensional poverty study.

Social protection coverage in and around Nsukka is real but uneven in both reach and adequacy. The National Home-Grown School Feeding Programme operates in every sampled school across Enugu metropolis, and most school administrators credit it with measurable reductions in hunger and improvements in attendance. However, the programme's per-child meal budget, absence of kitchen infrastructure, and irregular deworming schedule constrain its nutritional impact below its stated objectives. Enugu State's own Conditional Cash Transfer initiative, examined separately from the national programme, similarly demonstrated documented policy existence without commensurate beneficiary-reported improvement in living standards. At the national level, the clearest causal evidence available — drawn from a comparable Southeast/South-South Nigerian state rather than Enugu directly — indicates that the flagship cash transfer programme can coincide with worse, not better, food security outcomes among beneficiaries when transfer values are inadequate and disbursement is irregular, even as it improves financial inclusion and productive asset accumulation. Informal kinship and communal support systems continue to function as the de facto primary safety net where formal programming under-delivers.

Discussion

Taken together, the evidence indicates that formal social protection offers, at best, partial and uneven relief against deep structural poverty in Nsukka and the wider Enugu State context. High monetary and multidimensional poverty rates, corroborated by direct household-level food insecurity data from Nsukka itself, signal that safety nets alone are insufficient without complementary investment in education, health, agricultural productivity, and non-farm economic diversification. The Akwa Ibom cash transfer finding is the most analytically important result in this body of evidence precisely because it complicates a simple narrative of programme expansion as the policy solution: a cash transfer that is too small and too irregular to track inflation can leave beneficiaries worse off on the dimension it is meant to protect, even while generating real but secondary benefits in financial inclusion and group-based empowerment. The Sub-Saharan Africa-wide systematic review reinforces that this is not an isolated finding but a structural feature of poorly designed or under-resourced transfer programmes across the region.

A social work perspective situates these findings within a broader critique of safety-net design that treats coverage as a proxy for impact. Leveraging Igbo communal solidarity structures — the same informal networks that already absorb much of the welfare burden where formal programmes under-deliver — alongside accountable, integrated formal services, offers a more credible route to durable poverty reduction than scaling a single underfunded cash transfer mechanism. The implementation gaps documented in the Enugu school feeding programme (inadequate per-meal funding, absent kitchen infrastructure) and in Enugu's own CCT case study point toward the same underlying constraint identified in the rural Enugu LGA study: governance and delivery-system capacity, not the absence of policy frameworks, is the binding constraint. This reframes the policy problem from “does Nsukka have social protection coverage” to “is the social protection Nsukka receives adequate, reliable, and well-targeted enough to function as protection at all.”

Implications for Social Work Practice and Policy

Social workers can play a pivotal role in addressing the adequacy and delivery gaps identified above by:

1. Supporting community monitoring and advocacy to improve targeting accuracy and hold cash transfer and school feeding programmes accountable for both coverage and benefit adequacy, not coverage alone.

2. Developing hybrid “cash-plus” interventions that pair transfers with skills training, agricultural extension, and health-system linkages — mirroring the asset-building and cooperative-formation benefits that, even amid disappointing food security outcomes, the Akwa Ibom evaluation found cash transfer beneficiaries did experience.

3. Facilitating participatory needs assessments that amplify beneficiary voices, addressing the disconnect between stated programme objectives and the lived experience reported by Enugu CCT beneficiaries and Nsukka-area school administrators alike.

4. Fostering collaboration between government agencies, NGOs, and traditional/communal institutions, recognizing that informal kinship networks already function as Nsukka's primary safety net and should be resourced as partners rather than treated as a fallback for formal-system failure.

Policymakers should prioritize three things in tandem: expanding coverage in rural high-poverty zones like Nsukka; pegging transfer values to inflation-adjusted cost of living rather than fixed nominal amounts, directly addressing the mechanism identified in the Akwa Ibom evaluation; and investing in delivery infrastructure (kitchens, regular deworming logistics, disbursement reliability) so that programmatic presence translates into the welfare gains intended, in alignment with Sustainable Development Goal targets on poverty and food security.

Conclusion

Secondary and locally grounded primary data together affirm persistent and severe household poverty in Nsukka and Enugu State, with social protection programmes delivering real but incomplete coverage and, in at least one rigorously evaluated case, an outcome that ran counter to the programme's own stated objective. This is not an argument against social protection as a strategy, but evidence that coverage statistics alone are an unreliable guide to impact. Strengthening these systems through enhanced professional social work involvement — centered on empowerment, equity, adequacy of benefit, and resilience-building — offers a more promising route to sustainable poverty reduction in southeastern Nigeria than coverage expansion alone. Future research should prioritize primary, longitudinal data collection at the Nsukka LGA level specifically, both to update the now-outdated 2011 multidimensional poverty estimate and to test directly whether the adequacy and targeting problems identified in Akwa Ibom's cash transfer programme are also present in Enugu State's own implementation.
