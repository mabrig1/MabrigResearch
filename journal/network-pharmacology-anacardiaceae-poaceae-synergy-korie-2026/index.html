Abstract

Network pharmacology has become a standard lens for explaining why multi-component plant extracts so often outperform single isolated compounds: by examining how each constituent's documented molecular targets relate to one another, researchers can build testable, hypothesis-generating pictures of which proteins and pathways a given botanical combination most plausibly acts through, well before any new wet-laboratory or computational work begins. This article undertakes a preliminary investigation of a specific, previously unexamined combination — leaf-derived flavonoids and phenolics from the Anacardiaceae family (Mangifera indica, mango, and Anacardium occidentale, cashew) paired with bioactives from Poaceae leaf sources (Bambusa spp., bamboo) — built around five recurring bioactive compounds (mangiferin, quercetin, anacardic acid, orientin, and vitexin), conducted through an extensive synthesis of secondary, previously published pharmacological literature and descriptive chemical-database evidence rather than through new primary computation.

A central methodological note governs everything that follows and is stated here without qualification: this is a preliminary investigation built on secondary data synthesis, not a primary computational or experimental study. No new target-prediction queries, protein–protein interaction network construction, topological hub analysis, enrichment analysis, or molecular docking runs were independently performed by the author for this article. Instead, the compound–target associations, pathway convergences, and mechanistic claims set out in the Results section are drawn directly from, and attributed throughout to, an extensive body of already-published pharmacological literature documenting the individual activity of mangiferin, quercetin, anacardic acid, orientin, and vitexin, together with descriptive (non-numerical) information drawn from public chemical and pathway databases. Where the secondary literature is silent or where a claim would require a genuine network-construction or docking run to substantiate, this is stated explicitly rather than filled in with invented figures. The contribution of this article is the synthesis itself — a literature-grounded, citable, hypothesis-generating multi-target picture, together with a fully specified computational framework (Section 3.4) that the next researcher could execute to convert this preliminary synthesis into a primary, independently generated network pharmacology and docking study.

1. Introduction: Building a Multi-Target Hypothesis from What Is Already Known

Network pharmacology starts from a simple observation that classical, one-drug-one-target pharmacology is often poorly equipped to handle: most complex diseases, and certainly most chronic metabolic and inflammatory conditions, are not driven by a single defective protein but by a web of interacting genes, proteins, and signalling pathways acting together (Hopkins, 2008). A plant extract that contains dozens of bioactive compounds, each weakly engaging several targets at once, can in principle achieve a therapeutic effect that no single isolated compound engaging one target with high affinity could match — not because any one interaction is especially strong, but because the combination's targets converge on the same disease-relevant network nodes from several directions simultaneously (Wagner & Ulrich-Merzenich, 2009). This is, in computational language, exactly the logic that African and Asian traditional polyherbal medicine has followed for centuries on purely empirical grounds (Sofowora, 1993; Iwu, 1993; Gurib-Fakim, 2006).

Before any new database query, network construction, or docking run is undertaken for a previously unexamined botanical combination, it is good practice — and, for resource-limited research settings, often the only immediately affordable step — to first establish how much of a multi-target hypothesis can already be supported by what has been independently published about each candidate compound in isolation. Public compound and pathway databases (Kim et al., 2021; Mendez et al., 2019; Szklarczyk et al., 2023) and several decades of individual pharmacological studies on common flavonoids, xanthones, and phenolic lipids together constitute a substantial body of secondary evidence about which proteins and pathways compounds such as quercetin, mangiferin, and anacardic acid are documented to engage. Synthesising that existing evidence carefully, compound by compound, before committing to new primary computation, is the task this article undertakes.

This article applies that synthesis approach to a combination that — so far as a careful search of the published literature could establish — has not yet been examined as a unit: leaf-derived bioactives from two Anacardiaceae sources, Mangifera indica (mango) and Anacardium occidentale (cashew), considered alongside leaf-derived bioactives from a Poaceae source, Bambusa spp. (bamboo). All three plants are common, inexpensive, and widely available across Nigeria and much of tropical Africa and Asia, and all three have independent histories of traditional use for metabolic, inflammatory, and hepatic complaints. Five compounds recur across the published phytochemical profiles of these three botanical sources and anchor the synthesis that follows: mangiferin and quercetin (both well represented in mango leaf), anacardic acid (the principal phenolic lipid of cashew leaf and shell), and orientin and vitexin (C-glycosylflavones common to bamboo leaf and reported elsewhere in the bamboo-and-MASLD literature this journal has previously discussed; Korie, 2026).

It is necessary to be unusually explicit, at the very outset, about what kind of paper this is and is not. It is not a report of a new, independently executed network pharmacology study: no target-prediction database was freshly queried for this specific five-compound set as a unit, no protein–protein interaction network was constructed and analysed in Cytoscape or an equivalent tool for this article, no enrichment analysis was run, and no docking simulation was performed. It is, instead, a preliminary investigation that synthesises an extensive body of existing, previously published secondary evidence — documented target interactions, pathway associations, and bioactivity reports for each of the five compounds individually — into a single, integrated, hypothesis-generating multi-target picture for the combination as a whole, together with a fully specified computational framework (Section 3.4) for converting that preliminary picture into a primary, independently generated result.

Three tasks organise what follows. First, the conceptual and biological rationale for treating these five compounds as a candidate multi-target combination is laid out, drawing on the documented pharmacology of each compound individually. Second, the secondary data sources consulted and the synthesis approach used to integrate them are specified, alongside a complete computational framework that a future researcher could execute to generate new primary network and docking results. Third, the synthesis itself is presented — a literature-grounded, citable account of the targets and pathways through which this combination most plausibly acts, with every claim traced to its basis in existing secondary evidence — and the article closes with a concrete roadmap for converting this preliminary synthesis into a primary, independently validated study.

2. Conceptual Background: Five Compounds, Three Plants, One Network

2.1 Why Network Pharmacology Fits Multi-Plant Combinations

Classical pharmacology has historically rewarded selectivity: the ideal drug binds one target tightly and leaves everything else alone, because off-target binding is usually where side effects come from. Network pharmacology inverts that intuition for a specific class of problem — complex, multifactorial disease — by treating limited, distributed polypharmacology as a feature rather than a bug (Hopkins, 2008). A compound that binds ten different proteins weakly, each of which sits in a disease-relevant pathway, can in principle move a biological system further than a compound that binds one protein perfectly, provided the ten weak interactions are not simply redundant. Multi-plant combinations, by definition, multiply this effect: each additional botanical source contributes its own compound list, and therefore its own set of predicted targets, increasing both the breadth of pathway coverage and the analytical complexity of working out which targets actually matter.

2.2 The Five Anchor Compounds and Their Botanical Sources

Five compounds anchor the worked example developed in this article, chosen because each is well represented in the published phytochemical literature on the three plants under consideration and each belongs to a structural class — xanthones, flavonoids, and phenolic lipids — with an established record of multi-target pharmacological activity. Mangiferin, a C-glycosyl xanthone, is the signature compound of Mangifera indica leaf and has been studied extensively for antioxidant, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory activity. Quercetin, one of the most widely distributed flavonols in the plant kingdom and present in mango leaf alongside mangiferin, is among the most frequently modelled compounds in the network pharmacology literature precisely because of its broad, well-characterised target profile. Anacardic acid, a phenolic lipid distinctive to Anacardium occidentale (cashew) leaf, shell, and nut testa, has documented anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity attributed in part to its capacity to inhibit certain lipoxygenase and histone acetyltransferase enzymes. Orientin and vitexin, both C-glycosylflavones, are recurring constituents of Bambusa spp. leaf and were among the compounds implicated in the bamboo-leaf MASLD mechanism this journal has previously discussed (Korie, 2026).

2.3 Documented Targets and Pathways: A Compound-by-Compound Secondary Data Summary

Rather than waiting for a fresh target-prediction query, a substantial multi-target picture can already be assembled from what has been independently reported, compound by compound, in the existing pharmacology literature. Quercetin is among the most extensively studied flavonols in the literature and has been repeatedly reported to modulate PI3K/AKT signalling, to inhibit NF-κB-mediated inflammatory transcription (acting on the RELA/p65 subunit and downstream TNF- and IL-6-linked signalling), and to activate Nrf2 (NFE2L2)-dependent antioxidant response pathways. Mangiferin has been documented in numerous independent studies as an activator of AMPK (the PRKAA1-encoded catalytic subunit), with reported downstream effects on hepatic lipid metabolism and glucose handling consistent with this journal's earlier discussion of AMPK-centred mechanisms (Korie, 2026), alongside reported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Anacardic acid has a well-documented, comparatively distinctive mechanism among the five compounds: it has been reported in the biochemical literature as an inhibitor of histone acetyltransferase (HAT) enzymes and of certain lipoxygenase activity, underlying its documented anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. Orientin and vitexin, the two C-glycosylflavones common to bamboo leaf, have each been reported to exhibit antioxidant activity consistent with Nrf2 pathway engagement and anti-inflammatory activity consistent with attenuation of NF-κB and MAPK signalling, alongside the hepatoprotective and metabolic effects already discussed for bamboo-leaf bioactives elsewhere in this journal (Korie, 2026).

Read together, this compound-by-compound secondary evidence converges, independently of any new network construction, on a recurring small set of targets and pathways: AKT1/PI3K signalling, NF-κB-linked inflammatory signalling (TNF, IL6, RELA), Nrf2-dependent oxidative stress response (NFE2L2), and AMPK-centred metabolic signalling (PRKAA1), with MAPK1 and STAT3 appearing as plausible secondary nodes given their well-documented position downstream of the inflammatory and growth-factor signalling already implicated for these compound classes. This convergence is the product of literature synthesis, not of a newly constructed protein–protein interaction network — no degree, betweenness, closeness, or MCC score has been computed for any of these proteins in relation to this specific five-compound set, and none is reported here.

2.4 What Independent Computation Would Still Need to Establish

This secondary synthesis narrows the search space considerably but cannot, on its own, substitute for four things that this article does not supply: a genuine target-prediction query for each of the five named compounds, run as a set, against a current database such as SwissTargetPrediction (Daina et al., 2019); construction of the resulting protein–protein interaction network in STRING (Szklarczyk et al., 2023) and Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003) for this specific compound set; topological analysis to identify which targets actually occupy hub positions in that specific network, as opposed to recurring across the separate literatures on each compound individually; and molecular docking of each compound against the crystal structures of its candidate targets using a tool such as AutoDock Vina (Trott & Olson, 2010). Section 3.4 specifies exactly how each of these four steps should be carried out by a future researcher.

3. Methods: Secondary Data Sources, Synthesis Approach, and a Framework for Future Validation

This section first specifies how the secondary literature synthesis presented in Section 4 was conducted, then sets out — as a clearly separated, prospective protocol — the computational framework that a future researcher would need to execute in order to convert this preliminary synthesis into a primary, independently generated result.

3.1 Secondary Data Sources Consulted

The synthesis draws on two categories of secondary source. The first is the published pharmacological literature reporting documented molecular targets, signalling pathway effects, and bioactivity classes for each of the five compounds individually — mangiferin, quercetin, anacardic acid, orientin, and vitexin — accumulated over several decades of independent natural-products and biochemical research. The second is descriptive (non-numerical) information drawn from public chemical and pathway reference resources, principally PubChem (Kim et al., 2021), ChEMBL (Mendez et al., 2019), and the STRING and KEGG pathway reference frameworks (Szklarczyk et al., 2023; Kanehisa & Goto, 2000), consulted here only for general compound identity, structural class, and pathway-naming purposes rather than for any newly run target-prediction, network-construction, or enrichment query.

3.2 Synthesis Approach: From Individual Compound Reports to an Integrated Picture

For each of the five compounds, documented targets and pathway associations reported independently in the literature were catalogued, then cross-compared across the five compounds to identify targets and pathways that recur for more than one compound in the set — on the reasoning that convergent targets reported independently for several compounds in the combination constitute a stronger basis for a multi-target hypothesis than any single compound's profile considered alone. This is a qualitative literature-synthesis method, not a quantitative network-construction method: no protein–protein interaction network was built, and no topological centrality score was computed, for the recurring targets identified this way.

3.3 Evidentiary Standards Applied

Claims in Section 4 are presented using a deliberately conservative, qualitative vocabulary — "well documented," "reported in multiple independent studies," "documented," "reported" — calibrated to how consistently and how often a given target or pathway association recurs across the literature on a compound's broader structural class, rather than to any single study. Where a claim could not be grounded in this way, it is omitted rather than asserted. No precise numerical value (a binding affinity in kcal/mol, a centrality score, an enrichment p-value) is reported anywhere in Section 4, because no computation that would produce such a value was performed for this specific five-compound set; the synthesis is intentionally restricted to claims that secondary literature can actually support.

3.4 Computational Framework for Future Independent Validation

The remainder of this section specifies, as a prospective protocol, the steps a future researcher would need to follow to move beyond secondary synthesis and generate a primary, independently computed network pharmacology and docking result for this compound set. Every tool and database named below is real and publicly accessible at the time of writing. No claim is made that these steps were carried out for this article.

3.4.1 Compound and Target Data Acquisition

The framework begins with formal structural identification of each candidate compound by retrieving its canonical SMILES string and basic physicochemical descriptors (molecular weight, LogP, hydrogen bond donor and acceptor counts, topological polar surface area) from PubChem (Kim et al., 2021) or ChEMBL (Mendez et al., 2019). These descriptors support a basic drug-likeness screen against established medicinal-chemistry heuristics such as Lipinski's Rule of Five (Lipinski, Lombardo, Dominy, & Feeney, 2001) and are the required input format for target-prediction tools. Each compound's SMILES string is then submitted to a ligand-based target-prediction service — SwissTargetPrediction is specified here for concreteness (Daina et al., 2019) — restricting predictions to human protein targets and retaining hits above a stated probability or confidence threshold.

3.4.2 Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction

The union of all predicted targets across the compound set forms the input gene/protein list for network construction in the STRING database (Szklarczyk et al., 2023), restricted to Homo sapiens, with a minimum required interaction confidence score specified and reported explicitly (a "high confidence" threshold of 0.7 is standard practice). The resulting interaction network is exported and imported into Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003) for analysis and visualisation.

3.4.3 Topological Hub Analysis

Within Cytoscape, the CytoHubba or NetworkAnalyzer plugin (or equivalent) is used to compute degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality, and the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) score, for every node. Nodes are ranked by MCC score (or by a combination of measures, with the rule stated explicitly), and a pre-specified number of top-ranked nodes are carried forward as candidate hub targets.

3.4.4 Functional Enrichment Analysis

The full predicted-target list is submitted to Gene Ontology enrichment analysis (Ashburner et al., 2000) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000), using a tool such as the R package clusterProfiler (Yu et al., 2012). Results are filtered to a pre-specified, corrected significance threshold (adjusted p < 0.05 is standard) and reported with the number of contributing genes per term.

3.4.5 Molecular Docking Protocol

For each compound–hub-target pair, a three-dimensional structure of the target protein is retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (Berman et al., 2000). Docking is performed with AutoDock Vina (Trott & Olson, 2010), with the search space centred on the target's known or predicted binding site, repeated with several random seeds, and the lowest (most negative) binding energy reported in kcal/mol. Known active and inactive control ligands should be docked alongside the compounds of interest as a discriminative-validity check.

3.4.6 Validation and Reporting Standards

A complete report of any genuine run of this pipeline should disclose the database version and access date for every tool; the confidence and significance thresholds applied; redocking validation against any co-crystallised reference ligand with the resulting RMSD reported explicitly (commonly under 2.0 Å for an acceptable protocol); and a clear statement that docking scores are binding-affinity estimates, not measurements, requiring biochemical or cell-based confirmation. This framework, followed faithfully, would constitute a genuine primary network pharmacology and docking study. Section 4 is not that study; it is the preliminary, secondary-data-grounded synthesis that precedes it.

4.3 Pathway-Level Convergence Across the Combination

Reading Table 2 by column rather than by row makes the multi-target rationale for this combination explicit: TNF/IL-6/NF-κB-linked inflammatory signalling and Nrf2-dependent antioxidant response are each independently reported for all five compounds at "reported" strength or above, while AKT1/PI3K signalling and AMPK-centred metabolic signalling are each independently reported, at "reported" strength or above, for at least three of the five. This pattern of independently documented convergence across compound-specific literatures — quercetin and mangiferin contributing the strongest documented engagement with growth-factor/survival signalling, anacardic acid contributing a structurally distinctive enzyme-inhibitory mechanism, and the bamboo-derived orientin and vitexin contributing consistently to the oxidative-stress and inflammatory axes — is the central, literature-grounded hypothesis this article puts forward; it is offered as a synthesis of what is already documented, not as a network pharmacology result in its own right.

4.4 What Independent Network Construction and Docking Would Still Need to Confirm

The pattern in Section 4.3 is a hypothesis precisely because it has not been confirmed by a network built from this specific five-compound set, nor by docking any of the five compounds against any of the five targets/pathways discussed. A genuine STRING/Cytoscape network (Section 3.4.2–3.4.3) could surface additional or different hub targets not anticipated by this compound-by-compound literature synthesis; a genuine enrichment analysis (Section 3.4.4) could show that some of the documented associations summarised in Table 2, while individually real, do not in fact dominate the network topology once all predicted targets are considered together; and genuine docking (Section 3.4.5) could show that any one compound's predicted binding to a given target, however well documented qualitatively, is in fact weak relative to the others once an actual binding-affinity estimate is computed. None of these possibilities can be ruled out by literature synthesis alone.

4.5 What This Synthesis Would Mean, If Independently Confirmed

If the pattern in Section 4.3 were independently confirmed by the computational framework in Section 3.4, it would support a multi-target profile in which quercetin and mangiferin contribute the strongest engagement with growth-factor and survival signalling (AKT1) and inflammatory hub nodes (TNF/IL-6/RELA), anacardic acid contributes complementary anti-inflammatory engagement consistent with its documented enzyme-inhibitory activity, and the bamboo-derived C-glycosylflavones orientin and vitexin contribute relatively more toward the oxidative-stress and energy-sensing axis (NFE2L2/PRKAA1) — a division of labour across the three botanical sources that would echo, at the level of network pharmacology, the same complementary-mechanism logic this journal has previously discussed for a different bamboo-leaf combination (Korie, 2026). Whether this documented-literature pattern holds once a network is actually constructed and docking is actually run is precisely the question that executing the framework in Section 3.4 — and not this preliminary synthesis — would answer.

5. Discussion

5.1 Why Publish a Preliminary Synthesis Before New Computation

Network pharmacology and molecular docking studies have become common enough in the natural-products literature that the methodological template itself — which databases to query, in what order, with which thresholds — is now reasonably well established, even though individual papers rarely spell every step out in enough detail for independent replication, and fewer still make explicit how much of their eventual hub list could already have been anticipated from the separate literatures on each compound. Publishing a careful secondary synthesis first, decoupled from any newly computed result, serves a narrower but genuine purpose: it gives other researchers, particularly those working with limited computational resources or just beginning to apply these tools to African ethnobotanical material, a citable, literature-grounded starting hypothesis and a complete, named, reproducible protocol (Section 3.4) for testing it, rather than asking them to start from nothing. This is the same logic that justified publishing a literature-synthesis-only paper elsewhere in this journal (Korie, 2026): doing the preparatory, hypothesis-shaping work openly and honestly, clearly labelled as preparatory, before the resource-intensive primary computational work begins.

5.2 Keeping Secondary Synthesis Distinct from Primary Computation

Network pharmacology and docking studies carry a specific, well-documented risk: because the computational pipeline produces numbers — node counts, centrality scores, binding energies — that look identical in format whether or not the underlying analysis was actually run with care, it is comparatively easy for a literature synthesis like this one to drift into presenting its qualitative, secondary-source-derived claims as if they carried the same evidentiary weight as a freshly computed result. This article was deliberately structured to avoid that drift: Table 2 uses only qualitative documentation-strength labels ("well documented," "reported," "limited evidence reviewed") rather than any numerical centrality score, p-value, or binding energy, precisely because no computation that would produce such a number was performed for this compound set; and the methodological notice in the Abstract and the pull-quote preceding Section 1 repeat the same distinction in case a reader arrives at Section 4 without having read the earlier sections. Readers, reviewers, and anyone citing this work should treat that repetition as intentional rather than redundant.

5.3 Limitations

This article's limitations follow directly from its method: it is a synthesis of secondary literature, not a primary computational or experimental study, and its conclusions are only as strong as the literature search and synthesis judgement behind them. The literature search underlying Section 4 was conducted by a single author rather than through a systematic, pre-registered review protocol with independent dual screening, so omissions are possible; a compound–target association rated "limited evidence reviewed" in Table 2 may simply reflect a gap in the sources consulted rather than a genuine absence of documented activity. More fundamentally, no degree of literature convergence, however consistent, is a substitute for an actual target-prediction query, network construction, enrichment analysis, or docking run against this specific five-compound set — a genuine computational run, specified in Section 3.4, could surface a substantially different hub list or show that a qualitatively "well documented" association does not in fact dominate the network topology once all predicted targets are considered together.

5.5 Relevance to Nigerian and African Computational Pharmacology Capacity

Every tool named in Section 3 — PubChem, ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, STRING, Cytoscape, the relevant R or Python enrichment packages, AutoDock Vina, and the Protein Data Bank — is free to access and runs on modest computing hardware, which makes this kind of framework genuinely usable by researchers and graduate students working in Nigerian and other African institutions without specialist wet-laboratory funding or high-performance computing infrastructure. Mangifera indica, Anacardium occidentale, and Bambusa species are all common, inexpensive, and widely cultivated across Nigeria, which lowers the practical barrier to eventually pairing any computational findings with locally sourced extracts for the in vitro and in vivo confirmation work described in Table 3. Making the methodology explicit and freely reusable, rather than treating it as a black box behind a results table, is itself a small contribution toward building that capacity.

6. Conclusion

This article undertakes a preliminary investigation of multi-target synergy between Anacardiaceae leaf compounds (mangiferin and quercetin from Mangifera indica; anacardic acid from Anacardium occidentale) and Poaceae leaf compounds (orientin and vitexin from Bambusa spp.), built on an extensive synthesis of secondary, previously published literature documenting each compound's individual molecular targets and pathway associations, and specifies a complete computational framework for converting that synthesis into a primary result. It deliberately stops short of claiming any newly computed finding: no target-prediction query, network construction, enrichment analysis, or docking run was independently performed for this specific five-compound set, and every claim in Section 4 is traced to its basis in existing secondary literature for exactly that reason.

What this article offers instead is a citable, literature-grounded preliminary synthesis — showing that TNF/IL-6/NF-κB-linked inflammatory signalling and Nrf2-dependent antioxidant response are independently documented for all five compounds, and that AKT1/PI3K and AMPK-centred signalling are independently documented for at least three of the five — together with a named, step-by-step protocol (Section 3.4) for testing that hypothesis computationally, and a staged roadmap (Table 3) showing exactly what primary computational and laboratory work remains before any claim about this combination's actual multi-target mechanism could be responsibly made. Whether mangiferin, quercetin, anacardic acid, orientin, and vitexin genuinely converge on these targets within a single constructed network, and whether that convergence holds up under docking, is an open, answerable, and inexpensive question for the next researcher — in Nigeria or elsewhere — willing to run the framework in Section 3.4 for real.

Researchers wishing to execute the computational framework described in Section 3.4 should generate their own primary data using the named public tools and databases.
