Wanted but Untouched: Banditry, the Contested “ISIS Link,” and the Political Economy of Impunity in Northwest Nigeria

Abstract

On 14 November 2022, Nigeria's Defence Headquarters publicly named nineteen men as wanted terrorist and bandit kingpins operating across the country's Northwest, attaching a ₦5 million bounty to each. The list included Bello Turji — identified down to his alias and home village in Shinkafi, Zamfara State — and Ado Aliero. Three years later, both men remain at large, and Turji has continued to negotiate publicly with state officials, give interviews, and circulate audio messages naming the politicians he holds responsible for the region's insecurity. This persistence, alongside contested claims that Boko Haram, ISWAP, or the newer group Lakurawa have absorbed Northwest bandit networks into a transnational jihadist project, has produced public bewilderment: how can a state that knows a man's name, alias, and village fail, for years, to bring him to justice?

This paper synthesizes peer-reviewed scholarship and verified investigative reporting (2022–2025) on the so-called “ISIS link,” the documented careers of named bandit leaders, and the federal government's security-vote accountability gap, situating the findings within state capacity theory, political settlements theory, rentier/war-economy theory, and warlord-politics (“shadow state”) theory. It finds that the dominant evidence base does not support a wholesale jihadist takeover of Northwest banditry — bandit leaders have historically rebuffed both Boko Haram and ISWAP overtures — but that a narrower, more recent and still-contested convergence is visible at the margins through groups such as Lakurawa. More importantly, it argues that the persistence of named, located, yet unprosecuted bandit leaders is not best explained as simple state incapacity or individual presidential unwillingness, but as the predictable output of a political settlement in which security-vote discretion, subnational elite accommodation, and bandit self-financing interact to make sustained confrontation more politically costly than continued negotiation.

Introduction: The Puzzle of the Wanted List

On 14 November 2022, Nigeria's Defence Headquarters (DHQ) released a list of nineteen men it formally declared wanted as terrorist and bandit kingpins operating across the Northwest, placing a ₦5 million bounty on each (The Cable, 2022). The list was specific, not vague: Bello Turji was identified as “Bello Turji Gudda” of Fakai village, Shinkafi Local Government Area, Zamfara State; Ado Aliero was named alongside him. The declaration was meant to signal seriousness — a government putting names, faces, and addresses to an otherwise anonymized crisis. Yet by December 2025, Turji was not in custody. Instead, a circulated audio message had him publicly confirming that he had held peace talks with officials of the Zamfara State government during the administration of then-Governor Bello Matawalle — who would later be appointed Nigeria's Minister of State for Defence under President Bola Tinubu — denying he had been paid ₦30 million during those talks, and naming former governors of Zamfara and Sokoto States, along with a sitting senator, as the parties he held responsible for the insecurity engulfing the region (Sahara Reporters, 2025).

This juxtaposition — a formally wanted, individually identified kingpin conducting a public back-and-forth with the state through the press, three years after his name and bounty were announced — is the puzzle this paper addresses. It is not a question that can be answered by reference to any single official's character or will. It requires examining, in turn: how credible the claim is that Northwest banditry has been absorbed into a transnational jihadist (“ISIS”) project; what the documented careers of named bandit leaders actually show about the relationship between bandits and the state; what the political economy of Nigeria's opaque “security vote” system reveals about accountability incentives; how the current administration has responded to escalating mass-abduction events; and what established theories of state capacity, political settlements, conflict economies, and warlord politics suggest about why a state might persistently negotiate with, rather than prosecute, men it has already named.

The Contested “ISIS Link”: Jihadization or Banditization?

The clearest fieldwork-based evidence on this question comes from Barnett, Rufa'i, and Abdulaziz (2022), published in CTC Sentinel, the journal of the Combating Terrorism Center at West Point. Drawing on interviews with bandits and jihadi defectors, the authors document repeated attempts by jihadist groups to recruit Northwest bandit networks — and repeated rejections. Around 2014, the bandit leader Sheikh Halilu (Halilu Sububu) received an emissary from Boko Haram seeking to cement a partnership; Halilu turned the emissary away out of disinterest. By 2021, Bello Turji himself had received overtures by phone from ISWAP, which was looking to cement some form of cooperation; Turji denied any relationship with jihadists, telling interviewers, of his own gang, “we are not a religious movement” (Barnett et al., 2022). The authors' central, fieldwork-grounded conclusion is that Northwest bandits have largely resisted jihadist cooptation precisely because they had already become wealthy and powerful enough — through cattle rustling, kidnapping-for-ransom, and illegal mining — to not need a jihadist patron, and because their objectives are pecuniary rather than political or theological.

More recent scholarship complicates, without overturning, that picture. Okunola and Ayodele (2026), writing in Small Wars & Insurgencies, trace how armed groups in Sokoto State evolved from disorganized “romanticised” bandits lacking coordination and basic weapons into actors practicing “stationary banditry”: imposing illegal taxation, establishing parallel governance structures, and acquiring military-grade weapons through regional arms proliferation. They describe the 2024 emergence of the group Lakurawa in Sokoto — proscribed as a terrorist organization by the Nigerian government later that year — as marking a shift from purely criminal banditry toward what they term a “proto-jihadist frontier,” a process of “Sahelization” in which ideological insurgency hybridizes with criminal entrepreneurship, facilitated by collapsed local governance and cross-border sanctuaries shared with Mali and Niger. Even so, claims that Lakurawa constitutes a confirmed extension of Islamic State's Sahel or West Africa structures remain contested among analysts, and should not be conflated with the broader, longer-documented bandit economy of Zamfara, Katsina, and Kaduna, where the CTC Sentinel's finding of bandit resistance to jihadist absorption remains the best-supported account. The honest summary is therefore not that Northwest Nigeria's bandits are, or are not, “ISIS” — it is that a predominantly profit-driven criminal insurgency now coexists with a narrower, newer, and still-evolving zone of jihadist convergence at its geographic margins, and that conflating the two risks both overstating the threat and excusing the political failures documented in the remainder of this paper.

Named, Located, and Still at Large: The Anatomy of Impunity

The DHQ's November 2022 declaration was notable for its specificity. Of the nineteen men named, Ado Aliero had already been on a wanted list since 2019 — yet in July 2022, months before the DHQ bounty announcement, he was conferred the traditional chieftaincy title of “Sarkin Fulani” in Zamfara State (The Cable, 2022). The overlap between a wanted bandit leader and a state-recognized traditional institution, occurring before the formal terrorism declaration, illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout this region's recent history: formal accountability mechanisms operating on a different track, and timeline, from informal local accommodation.

Turji's December 2025 audio message, in which he confirmed direct peace engagement with the Matawalle-era Zamfara government, is not an isolated episode but the most recent documented instance of a cycle traced by The New Humanitarian (2025). Zamfara first experimented with bandit amnesty in 2016, brokering a truce with bandit leader Buharin Daji under then-Governor Abdulaziz Yari, in which fighters surrendered stolen cattle and a small number of weapons in exchange for cash. Successive northwest amnesty and peace-deal efforts since have followed a broadly repeated pattern: negotiation, partial demobilization, mutual accusations of bad faith, and a return to arms by most “repentant” bandits. The New Humanitarian's analysis concludes that poorly structured amnesty deals have not measurably reduced violence; instead, they have emboldened bandit groups and undermined the state's own claim to a monopoly on legitimate force, perpetuating rather than resolving the cycle of impunity.

What is striking about the December 2025 episode is the role-reversal it makes visible: a man the state has formally branded a terrorist using a public platform to accuse named former governors and a sitting senator of responsibility for the violence engulfing their states, and to call for their arrest and investigation (Sahara Reporters, 2025). Whatever the self-interested motives behind Turji's statement, its existence — a wanted kingpin publicly demanding accountability from politicians while himself evading it — is itself evidence of how thoroughly the boundary between state and armed-actor accountability has eroded in practice.

The Political Economy of Non-Confrontation: Security Votes and Accountability

Part of the explanation lies in how Nigeria's states fund their declared security responses. In November 2025, the Socio-Economic Rights and Accountability Project (SERAP) formally called on President Tinubu to establish a presidential panel of inquiry into allegations of embezzlement, misappropriation, and diversion of “security votes” by state governors dating back to May 2015 — spanning both the Buhari and Tinubu administrations (Vanguard, 2025, 27 November). SERAP's request cited an estimate, drawn from a Transparency International report, that security votes disbursed across Nigeria's 36 states amount to roughly ₦241.2 billion annually, with most of these funds reportedly spent on political activities, mismanaged, or stolen rather than applied to their stated security purpose.

The consequence of that accountability gap is measurable in the casualty and ransom data. SBM Intelligence's report on “The Economics of Nigeria's Kidnap Industry” documented 4,722 people abducted in 997 separate incidents nationwide between July 2024 and June 2025, with at least 762 people killed and an estimated ₦2.57 billion paid in ransom over the same period (Vanguard, 2025, 28 August). Read alongside SERAP's allegations, the figures suggest that the institutional apparatus nominally funded to prevent this violence — hundreds of billions of naira in annual security votes — is not translating into a reduced casualty or kidnapping toll, a disconnect that is difficult to attribute to resource scarcity alone.

Government Response Under President Tinubu: Emergency Measures Amid Negotiation Controversy

In late November 2025, following mass abductions of schoolchildren and civilians in Kebbi, Niger, and Kwara States, President Tinubu declared a nationwide security emergency and announced the recruitment of 20,000 additional police officers, intended to raise the total police force toward 50,000 (Punch, 2025, 26 November [emergency]). The declaration was a visible, structurally significant response — but it arrived alongside a parallel controversy that exposed the same accommodation pattern documented in the preceding sections.

Members of the National Assembly, in both the Senate and the House of Representatives, publicly and openly rejected the federal government's negotiations with bandits and kidnappers over the Kebbi and Kwara hostage-takings, characterizing the talks as a sign of weakness that would incentivize further kidnapping (Punch, 2025, 26 November [emergency]). Specifically, the House of Representatives, under a coalition styling itself “House to the Rescue,” condemned the federal government around 26 November 2025 for negotiating with bandits to secure the release of twenty-four students abducted from the Government Girls Comprehensive Secondary School in Maga, Kebbi State — a negotiation that presidential aide Bayo Onanuga publicly confirmed had occurred (Punch, 2025, 26 November [negotiating]). The coalition demanded that the federal government halt all negotiations with bandits, pursue intelligence-driven rescue operations instead, publish a national security strategy with built-in accountability mechanisms, and subject any official engaged in unauthorized talks with criminal groups to full National Assembly oversight. The episode captures, in miniature, the structural bind at the center of this paper: a government that negotiates for hostages because negotiation produces faster releases, even as doing so visibly rewards and legitimizes the very kingpins it has named as wanted.

Why States Fail to Confront Known Threats: A Theoretical Framework

State capacity theory offers a first, partial explanation. The puzzle of non-prosecution is rarely a puzzle of “not knowing” — it is a puzzle of coercive reach. Where a state's monopoly on legitimate force is contested across vast, sparsely governed rural, forest, and semi-desert terrain straddling international borders, as in Zamfara, Katsina, Sokoto, and Niger States, the state frequently substitutes negotiation or selective amnesty for capture and prosecution because it lacks the sustained logistics, intelligence penetration, and judicial reach required to apprehend and try mobile armed actors operating across that terrain (Okunola & Ayodele, 2026).

Political settlements theory, developed by Mushtaq Khan (2010), supplies a sharper account of why this capacity deficit persists rather than being closed. Khan argues that a state's formal institutions reflect the underlying distribution of power among contending political and economic factions — a “political settlement.” Where local power-holders, including politicians, security-vote beneficiaries, and traditional institutions, derive rents, protection, or political advantage from informal arrangements with armed groups, a decisive formal prosecution effort would destabilize the very settlement that holds the broader governing coalition together. This helps explain why even an administration with genuine prosecutorial intent encounters strong resistance from within its own coalition to a sustained crackdown: the costs of disruption are borne disproportionately by entrenched local actors who are also, often, indispensable political allies.

Rentier and war-economy theory, associated with Paul Collier (2000), adds the financial dimension. Cattle rustling, kidnapping-for-ransom, and illegal mining provide Northwest bandit groups with self-financing revenue streams that are independent of any state or jihadist patron. These revenue streams simultaneously fund the bandits' continued operational capacity and create parallel “taxation” and protection economies from which some local elites profit indirectly — reducing, rather than increasing, elite incentive for a costly and destabilizing confrontation.

Finally, William Reno's (1999) theory of warlord politics and the “shadow state” describes a governing strategy, not a governing failure: in weak states, rulers sometimes find it cheaper and more politically flexible to govern through informal brokers — including armed strongmen — than to invest in the formal bureaucratic and judicial capacity that confrontation would require. That framework predicts exactly the pattern documented above: officials who negotiate with, rather than fight, men they have themselves formally branded terrorists, because informal brokerage is, in the short run, the lower-cost option for managing an otherwise unmanageable security crisis.

Taken together, these frameworks suggest that the question animating public bewilderment — what could make a president incapable of, or unwilling to, solve a security crisis of this magnitude — is misframed if directed at any single officeholder. The verified record shows negotiation episodes with named bandit actors occurring under successive Zamfara state administrations and persisting into the federal government's own hostage negotiations in 2025. The pattern is one of institutional continuity across administrations, not a single leader's idiosyncratic failure. Breaking it would require a realignment of the underlying political settlement — disrupting security-vote discretion, subnational elite accommodation, and bandit self-financing simultaneously — that no single administration, to date, has demonstrated the willingness or the coalition-wide capacity to engineer.

Conclusion: Beyond Bafflement

The public bafflement at the heart of this inquiry — how a government can know a man's name, alias, and village, yet not prosecute him for years — rests on an implicit assumption that non-prosecution must reflect either simple incapacity or simple bad will. The evidence assembled here supports a more structural account. Named bandit leaders persist because the political economy of opaque, weakly audited security votes, the informal political settlements binding subnational elites to armed actors, the self-financing capacity of bandit groups through rustling, ransom, and illegal mining, and a genuine coercive-capacity deficit across contested terrain interact to make sustained, system-wide prosecution considerably more costly to the political order than continued negotiation and selective accommodation. The narrower but real possibility of jihadist convergence through groups such as Lakurawa raises the stakes of this equilibrium and may, over time, force a recalibration that bandit-only criminality has not.

Three evidence-grounded implications follow directly from the record reviewed above. First, SERAP's call for an independent, judicially accountable forensic audit of security-vote disbursement since 2015 deserves serious institutional follow-through, not only renewed advocacy. Second, the DHQ's wanted list requires a resourced, judicially independent prosecution track insulated from ad hoc state-level negotiation, so that a kingpin's formal designation as wanted is not, in practice, optional. Third, the “House to the Rescue” coalition's 2025 demand for mandatory National Assembly oversight of any negotiation episode with armed groups offers a concrete, already-proposed mechanism for closing the gap between public condemnation of bandit negotiations and their continued occurrence in practice.
